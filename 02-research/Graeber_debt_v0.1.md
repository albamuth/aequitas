## Why this matters to Aequitas

Graeber dismantles the origin story economics tells about itself — that money arose to fix the awkwardness of barter — and shows that **credit and debt came first, before coins and before barter.** That matters to Aequitas twice over: it removes the standard argument for why a transferable money is *necessary*, and it reframes Aequitas's whole debt chapter (Overview §2) as a return to something older than money, not a leap into the untried.

Bears on: the debt argument (Overview §2); the "double coincidence of wants" barter story used to justify money (see [Local-currency field record](../00-strategy/GLOSSARY.md#src-local-currency-experiments)); and the rule that credit is only a record and can never be transferred ([Foundations §A3](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.13.md#a3-non-fungibility)).

## Key findings

- **Debt and credit predate money; money predates barter.** Drawing on anthropology, Graeber argues that societies ran on *credit* — informal running tallies of who owes whom — long before coins appeared (~600 BCE). The tidy story where barter comes first, then money to lubricate it, is a "myth" economists inherited from Adam Smith, and there's no historical record of a society that actually worked that way.
- **Money began as a unit of account for debt**, not as a physical medium that replaced swapping goods.
- **Debt is moral and social — and, when made precise and enforceable, violent.** Turning a fuzzy social obligation into an exact, collectible debt, Graeber argues, historically required state-backed force. Markets and violence are entangled, not opposites.

## What we can use

- **The barter myth is the usual argument for why you need money — and Graeber kills it.** Economics justifies a transferable medium of exchange by pointing to the "double coincidence of wants" problem in barter (see [Local-currency field record](../00-strategy/GLOSSARY.md#src-local-currency-experiments)). If credit-tallies, not barter, were the historical baseline, that justification loses its footing — which clears ground for Aequitas's central move: a running *record* of who did and used what, with no medium of exchange at all. See [Foundations §A3](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.13.md#a3-non-fungibility).
- **Aequitas is old, not exotic.** Graeber's "informal running tallies of who owes whom" is remarkably close to what an Aequitas ledger *is* — an append-only record of physical give-and-take. Aequitas can honestly say it revives the oldest form of economic life, freed of the enforcement violence Graeber describes, because nobody is ever *owed* transferable credit.
- **It sharpens Overview §2.** The modern debt spiral (Overview §2) looks less like an eternal feature of economies and more like an artefact of *money-denominated, enforceable* debt — exactly the thing Aequitas removes.

## What it gets wrong / limitations — from Aequitas's angle

- **Graeber describes; he doesn't build.** *Debt* is history and anthropology, not a mechanism. It clears intellectual ground but supplies no accounting system — Aequitas provides that.
- **His enforceable-debt-needs-violence point cuts both ways.** It's a caution for Aequitas too: any system that makes obligations *precise* risks needing coercion to collect. Aequitas's answer is that its "debt" is a physical fact carried by whoever holds the object or caused the harm — not a claim one person can enforce against another — so there is no collector and no debtor to coerce. Worth stating explicitly against Graeber's warning.
- **Some economists dispute his history.** The anthropology of "no barter economies ever existed" is contested at the edges. Cite the *structural* point (credit is at least as old as money) rather than the strongest version.
