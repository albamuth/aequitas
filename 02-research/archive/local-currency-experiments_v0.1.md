## Why this matters to Aequitas

`02-research/warren-cost-the-limit-of-price.md` and `02-research/proudhon-mutualism.md` cover the *doctrinal* ancestors. This note covers the *field record* — roughly a century of people actually building these things and finding out what breaks. It is the closest thing the project has to an experimental literature.

The working hypothesis going in was that Ithaca and Burlington are Proudhon/Warren problems in modern dress. **That is half right and the other half is more useful.** Warren and Proudhon died of a *valuation* problem. Ithaca and Burlington died of a *circulation* problem, which is a different failure and one Aequitas is structurally immune to — for a reason worth stating out loud. Meanwhile the time-banking literature supplies **direct empirical confirmation of the valuation problem**, at a scale and recency Warren cannot provide.

## Three distinct failure classes

The field record does not show one failure repeated. It shows three.

### Class 1 — Valuation: the flat hour cannot recruit skill

- **Warren (1827–30):** could not reconcile labour-for-labour with differences in skill and disagreeableness; ended by conceding it was "a matter of individual judgment."
- **Time banking (1980– ):** Cahn's Time Dollars value **every hour identically regardless of who contributes it**. The [2025 PLOS One scoping review](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0322760) finds the predictable result — **skill mismatch**: particular skills stay in short supply, there is no guarantee the bank meets anyone's actual demand, and the review also documents **credit hoarding** and limited recognition of diverse skills as barriers to participation.

**This is the finding with the most force in the whole note.** Warren's failure can be waved away as one man in one storefront in 1830. Time banking is *forty-five years, dozens of countries, a peer-reviewed literature* — and flat-hour crediting still fails to recruit skilled labour. **The objection to A2 is no longer theoretical.**

Aequitas's answer is genuinely different from time banking's and should be stated precisely: skill is not credited at a higher rate, but **training cost flows downstream into the recipient's debit** (A2). The doctor's hour costs the patient more than the gardener's hour, while both are credited one hour per hour.

**That fixes the price side and leaves the supply side open.** Why undertake ten years of training if the hour credits identically afterward? **OP-11 (training amortization) and OP-16 (the onerousness gap) are the same wound**, and time banking is the field evidence that it is fatal untreated.

### Class 2 — Circulation: currency pools at sinks and stops

This is what actually killed both cases the user named, and it is the same mechanism in each.

- **Ithaca HOURS.** Founded November 1991 by Paul Glover, pegged at **1 HOUR = US$10** (the average hourly wage in Tompkins County). Peak: **1,000+ individuals and 500 businesses**, including Cayuga Medical Center and Alternatives Federal Credit Union; several million dollars' value traded. As the directory shrank, the businesses that remained **"were drowning in Hours"** — receiving far more than they could spend, accumulating backlogs.
- **Burlington Bread.** Ran 1997–2007, up to **20,000 "slices"** in circulation. The unresolved problem, in the project's own account: Bread **piled up at a few food-related businesses** — Muddy Waters, Sugar Snap — which had no way to recirculate or redeem it.

The mechanism is a **topological** one, not a valuation one. Scrip flows toward businesses whose own inputs come from *outside* the network — a café buys coffee from Colombia in dollars. Those businesses are one-way sinks. Circulation halts, and it halts fastest at exactly the enthusiastic early adopters.

**Contrast the two systems that did not die:**

| | Ithaca / Burlington | WIR / Sardex |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | B2C retail margin | **B2B, dense input loops** |
| Money form | Issued paper scrip | **Mutual credit — balances start at zero, created in the act of trade** |
| Sink risk | High — retailers buy inputs outside | Low — businesses buy from each other |
| Result | Dead in ~15 and ~10 years | **WIR 1934–present, ~60,000 businesses, CHF 1.43bn (2013); Sardex 4,000+ businesses, ~€50m/yr** |

WIR is also demonstrably **countercyclical** — it expands when francs are scarce, which is why it has survived ninety years. Sardex was explicitly modelled on early zero-interest WIR.

### Class 3 — Institutional: evangelists, states, and obsolescence

- **Founder dependency.** Ithaca's decline is attributed first to **Glover relocating**. Glover himself said every local currency needs at least one full-time networker to "promote, facilitate and troubleshoot."
- **Legal suppression.** Wörgl's stamp scrip (1% monthly demurrage, per Gesell) cut unemployment **16% while Austrian unemployment rose 19%**, funded a reservoir, houses, a bridge and a ski jump — and was **terminated by the Oesterreichische Nationalbank on 1 September 1933** to protect the legal-tender monopoly. **It was killed for working.**
- **Technological obsolescence.** HOURS was paper and never made the transition to electronic payment; a 2019 digital revival failed.

## What we can use

**1. A3 is a structural immunity to Class 2, and it is currently unclaimed.**

There is no sink problem in a system with **no medium of exchange**. Credit is non-fungible and never moves (A3); only debit moves, and only attached to the thing it belongs to. Nobody can drown in credit they cannot spend, because nobody ever receives credit *from* anyone. The failure that ended both Ithaca and Burlington **cannot occur in Aequitas**, and this is a consequence of an existing axiom rather than a new mechanism. Say it in §7.

**2. The Wörgl lesson vindicates the terminology rule.**

Wörgl was suppressed because it was a **currency** competing with legal tender. Aequitas is an accounting system with no issuer, no notes, and nothing to counterfeit — so the legal instrument that killed Wörgl does not fit it, and there is no office to raid. `CLAUDE.md` already bans calling Aequitas a currency; **this is the historical reason, and it is a strategic argument, not just a branding preference.**

**3. WIR/Sardex give the MVP a target shape.**

Both survived by starting **B2B in dense input loops**, not with retail consumers. Both are strongly countercyclical — adoption rises when conventional money is scarce. If Foundations §11 ever needs a first *real* deployment rather than an overlay, **a dense B2B supply-chain cluster is the shape the field record endorses**, and a downturn is the moment.

**4. "Does this need a Paul Glover?" — a fourth screening question.**

Criterion 3 (fecundity) is precisely the test Ithaca failed: it did not encourage its own maintenance, it required a full-time evangelist, and it died when he moved. This is a sharper, more concrete form of the fecundity test and should be applied to every proposed mechanism. The existing trust model — **auditing as credited work** — is the right *shape* of answer, because it pays the maintainer from inside the system.

## What it gets wrong / limitations — and where Aequitas is exposed

1. **Every one of these was pegged to, and parasitic on, fiat.** One HOUR was *defined* as $10; Bread mirrored dollars in slices. They were never independent units of account — they were national currency with a local-loyalty restriction, which is why they added little and died quietly. **Foundations §11 proposes a "parallel overlay on existing commerce," which is a hair's breadth from the same trap.** The saving difference is that Aequitas's overlay computes a number money *cannot* produce — true debit-cost. **§11 should state that as its explicit rationale**, to distinguish it from the entire failed complementary-currency lineage rather than resembling it.

2. **Aequitas has an isomorph of the sink problem, one level up.** Immunity to *circulation* sinks is not immunity to *saturation*. A high producer eventually accumulates credit that raises a ceiling they are nowhere near — marginal credit that does nothing. Ithaca's businesses were drowning in HOURS they could not spend; Aequitas's most productive people can drown in headroom they do not want. Capitalism's answer to this is accumulation, which A3 forbids by design. Enrichment (§6.3) is the intended answer, which makes it **load-bearing as the motivation system for high producers, not a decorative third credit type**. Registered as **OP-19**; it also promotes **OP-6**.

3. **Small-n matching failure.** Time banks and LETS both suffer the double-coincidence problem: below some density, the thing you need is not offered. Aequitas is less exposed — it prices rather than matches — but the **Level 1 / Level 4 interop rule (§4) and cross-level trade (OP-7) inherit it**, because a small Level 1 community is exactly a small-n network.

4. **Credit hoarding is documented in time banks.** Worth noting against the assumption that non-transferability plus no interest removes all hoarding incentive. It does not; people bank hours against future need. Bears on OP-4 (debit tolerance).

5. **Scale is unproven everywhere.** The largest success here is WIR at ~60,000 businesses inside a functioning franc economy. Nothing in the field record tests a whole economy, and Aequitas should not cite these as evidence that it scales — only as evidence about **failure modes**.
