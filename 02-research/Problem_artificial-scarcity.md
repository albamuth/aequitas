# Is scarcity artificial? Six sources, and a test that can fail

**Version:** 0.1
**Type:** research note (a summary of six notes in this folder, with a proposal for one simulation)
**Author(s):** project note
**Retrieved:** 2026-11-19
**Read:** the six notes below, each read whole. Each note says what its writer opened.
**Local copy:** none

## The question

**The author's claim, 2026-11-19.** Scarcity today is largely artificial. Humanity can make more than it consumes. A few people hoard much of it. Much is wasted on war, luxury for the few, planned obsolescence (making a thing fail or date early), surplus dumping and needless cheap goods. People are conditioned to think that there is not enough to go around.

**The project's own measurement, Foundations section 3.5.** A typical life of the United States for all 8.1 billion people needs about 10.4 trillion hours of work a year. About 6.5 trillion are available. That is 0.63. By the methods of Germany, Japan, France and Sweden it is 0.72 to 0.75.

**These two pull in different directions.** This note says what six sources add, and it proposes the measurement that would settle it.

**Three of the six are weak sources.** Letteboer and Papadogiannis are draft papers by independent writers, and nobody here read their full text. Ayoki is not reliable.

## What the six support

- **Some scarcity is made by rules, and writers on every side say so.** Hutt (1936) and Boudreaux (2016) are free-market writers. Tucker (1888) and Kropotkin (1892) are anarchists. Lehr is an economist at MIT. Desai and Lemley are law professors. This backs the line in Foundations section 4.6 that much scarcity is produced.
- **Two cases with digits.** Brazil burned 6,565,641 sacks of coffee by June 1932, and the price rose about 40%. The Phoebus cartel of lamp makers cut the life of a lamp from 1,800 hours in 1926 to 1,205 hours in 1933–34, and fined a factory whose lamps lasted longer. Sources are in the [thread note](History_artificial-scarcity-thread.md).
- **An exclusive right makes a shortage of a thing that nobody made, or that copies freely.** A patent, a copyright, a spectrum licence and a land title all work this way. Aequitas has no patents (Foundations section 4.5) and no owned land (section 3.7).
- **On food, enough is grown.** Desai and Lemley report enough calories in 2012 for everyone and 1.6 billion more people. Their source is one teaching text, and nobody here opened it. The fact sheet of the Overview draft holds four checked numbers on food waste and hunger.

## What the six do not support

- **None measures how much scarcity is made.** No source gives a share of hours, a share of output, or a count of people. "Largely" has no number behind it in any of them.
- **None shows that the world can make more than it consumes.** Every source that looks at physical things says some shortage is real: energy, materials, power lines, land, good radio frequencies, a skilled person's time.
- **None documents hoarding by a few with figures.** The sources document restriction. Hutt's main culprit was the trade union. The crop destroyers were governments.
- **War, luxury, surplus dumping and cheap goods are almost absent.** Only the thread note has cases of destroyed surplus, from 1932 and 1933.
- **A shortage can have no culprit.** Letteboer's own test puts a shortage from poor coordination or missing information outside "artificial". Nobody holds anything back in such a case.
- **Ending a made shortage did not share out the gain.** When ride services ended the limit on taxis, riders paid less and drivers lost security (Desai and Lemley).

## Three kinds of shortage

Letteboer's test gives a way to sort one shortage from another. It is the most useful thing in the six.

| Kind | The test | Example | What Aequitas does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Natural** | It stays under good rules and reasonable investment | Energy today. Low radio frequencies. One seafront | A network sets a low tolerance (the share of a person's counted hours that their consumption may reach), and a queue or a lottery gives out the last one (Foundations sections 5.5.3 and 4.6) |
| **Made** | Capacity exists, a rule restricts access, and a named party gains | A patent on a medicine. Burned coffee | The cost rule removes the gain: holding supply back cannot move a cost (rule A5). There are no patents (section 4.5) |
| **Nobody's** | Capacity exists, and nobody gains from the shortage | Food that rots because nobody knew who wanted it | Pledges show makers what people want, before the thing is made (section 4.6) |

## A test that can fail

**The claim "capacity exceeds consumption" is true only if enough of today's work is waste that can be cut.** Three thresholds follow from numbers the project holds.

| The question | The arithmetic | Share of hours that must be waste |
|---|---|--:|
| Can the United States meet its own wants? | Foundations section 5.5.3: by the US method the economy delivers 85% of wants, at 13.80 hours a unit. 13.80 × 0.85 = 11.73 hours a unit delivers 100% | **15%** |
| Can the world live a typical US life, by the methods of four peer nations? | Need 8.7 to 9.1 trillion hours, and 6.5 trillion are available | **25% to 29%** |
| The same, by the US method | Need 10.4 trillion hours. (10.4 − 6.5) ÷ 10.4 | **37.5%** |

**Worked for one person.** A typical US life takes 1,380 hours a year. 37.5% of 1,380 is about 518 hours. So the strongest form of the claim holds only if 518 of the 1,380 hours are waste that can be cut with no loss.

**Energy is a separate limit.** Section 3.5 puts energy at 0.19 of what is needed. A cut in wasted hours does not close that by itself.

## The waste sim: a proposed design

*(Author, 2026-11-19: "The wastefulness of each sector must be the basis of a new sim for calculating what values of rho are plausible.")* Rho is the symbol for the tolerance.

**Why a new run is needed.** Every tolerance in Foundations section 5.5.3 is measured at a method that includes the waste. The edge of 0.330 at a 10-hour baseline is the US method as it is today.

**The inputs.** The data note [`Data_sector-waste.md`](Data_sector-waste.md) holds one row for each sector: a low and a high share of that sector's hours that are waste, each with a source.

**Three classes of waste, kept apart.** They are different things, and adding them hides that.

| Class | What it is | Example | What cutting it changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | A product that was made and thrown away | Food. US homes threw away 23.4 million tons in 2024 | The same life takes fewer hours |
| **B** | Work that exists only because of money | Lending, insurance, billing, advertising | The same life takes fewer hours. A network's own work has to be added back (section 4.7) |
| **C** | Spending that is a choice about the standard of living | Armed forces. Luxury above a typical life | The standard changes. This is a choice and not a saving |
| **D** | People who want work and have none | The unemployed. Part-timers who want full time | More hours are worked. These hours are inside the project's own band of hours, so they fix a point in the band and are never added to it |

**The rows are collected.** [`Data_sector-waste.md`](Data_sector-waste.md) has 13 rows from eight detail notes, five rules against counting twice, and a first look at the totals. In that first look, classes A and B come to about 6% of hours in the low case and about 17% in the high case, without food and clothing.

**What the run reports.** Hours a unit, and capacity as a share of wants, with A, with A and B, and with A, B and C, each at low and high. Then the tolerance at each baseline, or "no edge" where capacity is above wants. Then the world ratio beside 0.63, and energy beside 0.19.

**Four traps, named before the run.**

1. **The inputs can decide the answer.** Whoever picks the waste shares picks the result. Section 3.5 excludes a check like that. So each share comes from a published source with a low and a high value, written down before the run. A sector with no source runs at 0, and the report lists it.
2. **The comparison with Germany already counts some waste.** Germany makes the same standard on 0.836 of US hours. Part of that gap is the waste this run measures. The run must not count it twice.
3. **Cheap supply makes new demand.** Desai and Lemley give the cases. The run holds wants fixed, and it says so.
4. **Class B is not free to remove.** A trust network has auditors and estimators. Their hours go back in.

## What a public document can say today

- **It can say:** much scarcity is made by rules, with the coffee and the lamps as cases. Enough food is grown, and hunger comes from how food is shared and wasted. Aequitas removes the gain from holding supply back.
- **It cannot say yet:** that the world can make more than it consumes, or that scarcity is largely artificial. The waste sim is the measurement that could support either sentence.
- **It should not cite:** Ayoki for anything. Letteboer or Papadogiannis for a number.

## Gaps this found in Aequitas

| Gap | Where it came from |
|---|---|
| **No rule for radio spectrum.** It is like land for exclusion, and unlike land because a band is whole again when the transmitter stops | Lehr |
| **Nothing stops a network from limiting who may do a job, or from refusing outside goods** | The thread note |
| **A maker may still gain from selling more short-lived goods.** The cost rule removes the margin on each sale. Nobody has checked for another gain | The thread note, on the lamp cartel |
| **No rule for sharing a plant's output while a second plant is built** | Papadogiannis |
| **No answer to a state that blocks a supply chain** | Papadogiannis |
| **If machines replace most work, a thing costs very few hours, and the tolerance is the only brake** | Ayoki. The evidence is too weak to act on now |

## What was wrong in the summaries we were given

- **Ayoki:** labour hours are not a limit in the model. Hours of work collapse and output grows.
- **Lehr:** "physically plentiful" is not in the paper. He wants more open spectrum beside licences, and he supports auctions.
- **The FEE essay** is about 113 words: one quotation from Hutt and two sentences. It is not a survey.
- **The Stanford volume** does not say that scarcity ends. Open-source software is a small example in it.
