## Why this matters to Aequitas

Aequitas's whole idea of attribution rests on one phrase: costs are pinned to *"the people who caused the movement"* of material and energy ([Foundations §A1](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.13.md#a1-materialism-of-cost)). That phrase carries an enormous amount of weight and currently has no deeper justification behind it. **Ellerman supplies exactly that justification — and, in the same move, shows its hardest limit.** He is both the best philosophical ally the project has and the source of one of its live open problems (splitting blame within a team).

## Key findings

- Ellerman's "labour theory of property" is just an everyday legal principle applied to production: **assign responsibility to whoever was actually responsible.** This isn't a claim about who *deserves* what; it's the same rule courts already use for crimes, carried over to the workplace.
- The argument runs: only human beings *act* — tools and machines don't decide anything. So responsibility for what a factory produces belongs entirely to the people working there, and *not* to the machinery or the people who own it. (In his terms: if labour is in fact responsible, it should in law be responsible.)
- **Some things about a person can't actually be handed over — only pretended to be.** Your capacity to *act* is one of them. On this basis Ellerman argues the employment contract is invalid in the same structural way a voluntary-slavery contract is: not because it's unfair, but because the thing it claims to transfer (your agency) *cannot physically move to someone else.*
- He separates the **real firm** (the people actually doing the producing) from the **legal firm** (whoever the law says pockets the profit). No property right links them — only a contract does, and its direction is arbitrary: labour could just as well hire capital as the other way round.
- He is careful to distinguish this from Marx's labour theory of *value*. Ellerman's is a theory of **who's responsible**, not a theory of *price*.

## What we can use

1. **Adopt Ellerman as the grounding for attribution, in place of Marx.** This is much better positioning: it's a *legal-responsibility* argument, not a theory of value, so it doesn't drag in the century of technical problems and hostility attached to Marx's value theory. It's also harder to dismiss — it appeals to a principle its opponents already accept everywhere else.
2. **It reaches Aequitas's anti-capitalist conclusion by a second, independent route.** Aequitas already gets "no income from merely owning capital" from its price-equals-cost rule ([Foundations §7.1](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.13.md#71-capitalism-cannot-function)); Ellerman gets there from responsibility. Two independent roads to the same conclusion make a far stronger case than one.
3. **It grounds the "credit can't be transferred" rule directly.** Credit records who was *responsible*, responsibility is a fact about a specific person, and a fact about a person can't be handed to anyone else. So non-transferability ([Foundations §A3](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.13.md#a3-non-fungibility)) stops being a design *choice* and becomes a *consequence* — a real gain.
4. It gives the clean reply to "why do the machine's owners get nothing?" — because machines don't act, and responsibility belongs only to those who do.

## Where Aequitas is exposed

**The one serious problem — and it is serious.** Ellerman is explicit that responsibility for something a *team* jointly makes is **shared and cannot be cleanly split.** The whole crew is responsible for the whole bridge. There is no fact of the matter that says the welder caused 40% of it and the surveyor 15%.

But Aequitas needs *some* number for every collaborative event in its records.

This is the same shape as the problem one level up — when a single *process* makes several products, how do you split its cost? (See [Joint-production problem](../00-strategy/GLOSSARY.md#src-joint-production-allocation-problem).) There, Aequitas has a physical answer: it measures where the process actually sent its inputs. Here there's no physical trace to measure — a team's *effort* doesn't leave one. So this stays a genuinely open problem: splitting *blame* within a team (the *credit* half is settled — everyone is simply credited their own hours). See [Foundations §10](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.13.md#10-open-problems).

The direct upshot: **"hours worked" is a chosen convention, not a measurement.** It may well be the *right* convention — it's simple, universal, and hard to game in non-obvious ways — but it should be labelled honestly as a convention rather than dressed up as a physical fact. Aequitas already does this: the split of a joint job by hours is declared a *named convention*, not a measurement (see [Foundations §1.1 (named conventions)](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.13.md#11-named-conventions)).

Secondary limitations:
- Ellerman's own conclusion is **worker cooperatives** — a specific institutional recipe. Aequitas should take his *principle* and leave his institution, exactly as it takes Neurath's method while leaving Neurath's central planning.
- He says nothing about how a co-op should divide its surplus *internally* — which is precisely the team-splitting question Aequitas is left holding.
