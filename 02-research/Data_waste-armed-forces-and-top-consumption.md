**What this note is for.** It holds the class C rows for the waste run designed in [Problem_artificial-scarcity.md](Problem_artificial-scarcity.md). The rule for a row is in [Data_sector-waste.md](Data_sector-waste.md). Class C is spending that is a choice about the standard of living. This note covers two sectors: armed forces, and consumption by the richest households above a typical life.

**BLS** is the US Bureau of Labor Statistics. **CE** is its Consumer Expenditure Survey. **DoD** is the US Department of Defense. **SIPRI** is the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute. **SCF** is the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances.

### The arithmetic for the armed forces share

**United States labour force.** The BLS counts 170,807,000 people in the civilian labour force in 2025 ([Current Population Survey, table 1](https://www.bls.gov/cps/cpsaat01.htm), opened). That count leaves out active-duty military. 170,807,000 + 1,298,000 = **172,105,000**. The 2025 figure is an average of 11 months. October was not collected.

| Case | Sum | Jobs | Share of 172,105,000 |
|---|---|--:|--:|
| **Low** | 1,298,000 active + 789,000 civilians + 494,000 defence jobs at aerospace and defence firms | 2,581,000 | **1.5%** |
| Middle | 1,298,000 + 789,000 + 1,100,000 defence industrial base | 3,187,000 | 1.9% |
| **High** | 997 billion dollars × 5 jobs for each 1 million dollars | 4,985,000 | **2.9%** |
| Check on the high case | 1,298,000 + 760,000 reserves + 789,000 + 2,200,000 | 5,047,000 | 2.9% |
| Not used | 997 billion dollars × 6.9 jobs | 6,879,000 | 4.0% |

**Why 6.9 is not used.** The 6.9 comes from a 2017 paper and older dollars. A dollar bought more work then. Applied to 2024 dollars it counts too many jobs.

**One cross-check.** Peltier's 2025 page says DoD is 60% of 3,669,000 federal workers in 2024. 0.60 × 3,669,000 = 2,201,000. The rows above give 1,298,000 + 789,000 = 2,087,000. The two agree within 6%.

**Germany.** The labour force is about 47.5 million in 2025. That figure is from a reprint of a Destatis (German statistics office) release, seen in a search summary: [reprint](https://www.anwaltonline.com/arbeitsrecht/nachrichten/2165/erwerbstaetigenzahl-im-jahr-2025-fast-unveraendert/) (link not verified).

- Low: 184,200 + 82,000 + 105,000 = 371,200. 371,200 ÷ 47,500,000 = **0.78%**.
- High: 184,200 + 82,000 + 60,200 + 450,000 = 776,400. 776,400 ÷ 47,500,000 = **1.63%**.

**In plain words.** By the same count, defence work takes about twice the share of the labour force in the United States (1.5% to 2.9%) that it takes in Germany (0.78% to 1.63%).

## The arithmetic for the top fifths

**Source.** BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey, 2025, published 2026-10-29. A "consumer unit" is the survey's word for a household. Households are ranked by income before taxes and cut into five equal groups.

|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Lowest | 27,770 | $37,935 | 1.5 | 1,053.5 | 9.3% | 9.3% |
| Second | 27,543 | $51,405 | 2.1 | 1,415.8 | 12.5% | 12.6% |
| **Middle** | 27,778 | **$68,983** | 2.5 | 1,916.2 | 17.0% | 16.9% |
| Fourth | 27,353 | $92,250 | 2.9 | 2,523.3 | 22.3% | 22.4% |
| Highest | 27,551 | $159,512 | 3.2 | 4,394.7 | 38.9% | 38.8% |
| **All** | 137,994 | $81,905 | 2.4 | **11,303.5** | 100% | 100% |

Each group total is households × average spending. Example: 27,551 thousand × $159,512 = 4,394.7 billion dollars. BLS prints a total of 11,296.8 billion dollars. The gap of 6.7 billion is rounding in the averages.

**The cut, per household (the high value).**

- Fourth fifth: 27,353 thousand × ($92,250 − $68,983) = 27,353 thousand × $23,267 = **636.4 billion dollars**.
- Highest fifth: 27,551 thousand × ($159,512 − $68,983) = 27,551 thousand × $90,529 = **2,494.2 billion dollars**.
- Sum: 636.4 + 2,494.2 = **3,130.6 billion dollars**.
- Share of the total: 3,130.6 ÷ 11,303.5 = **27.7%**.

The highest fifth alone gives 2,494.2 ÷ 11,303.5 = 22.1%.

**The same cut, per person.** Richer households are larger: 3.2 people in the highest fifth against 2.5 in the middle. Spending per person is $27,593 in the middle fifth, $31,810 in the fourth and $49,848 in the highest.

- Fourth fifth: 79,324 thousand people × ($31,810 − $27,593) = 334.5 billion dollars.
- Highest fifth: 88,163 thousand people × ($49,848 − $27,593) = 1,962.0 billion dollars.
- Sum: 2,296.5 billion dollars. 2,296.5 ÷ 11,303.5 = **20.3%**.

The survey prints people per household to 1 decimal place, so this figure is rough.

**The same cut, with pension payments left out (the low value).** The survey counts payments into pensions and Social Security as spending. They are saving and tax, and they are not consumption. They come to 1,320.8 billion dollars, and the highest fifth makes 56.1% of them. With them left out, the total is 9,977.3 billion dollars.

|---|--:|--:|
| Middle | $62,405 | $24,962 |
| Fourth | $80,923 | $27,905 |
| Highest | $132,198 | $41,312 |

- Per household: 506.5 + 1,922.9 = 2,429.4 billion dollars. 2,429.4 ÷ 9,977.3 = **24.3%**.
- Per person: 233.4 + 1,441.5 = 1,674.9 billion dollars. 1,674.9 ÷ 9,977.3 = **16.8%**.

|---|--:|

**The top 10%.** Table 1110 gives the highest tenth of households 23.6% of all survey spending. 0.236 × 11,296.8 = 2,666.0 billion dollars across 13,798 thousand households, which is $193,219 per household. At the middle fifth's $68,983 the total falls by 1,714.2 billion dollars, which is **15.2%**. Per person, with 3.3 people per household, it falls by 1,409.6 billion dollars, which is **12.5%**.

**The top 1%.** The CE does not publish a top 1% group. The Federal Reserve note gives the 1% of families who spend the most 9% of all spending in the SCF. If those families spent an average amount, they would make 1% of spending. Total spending would fall by about 8%. This is ranked by spending and not by income, so it cannot be added to the rows above.

**A check against the project's own figure.** The middle fifth spends $68,983 against an average of $81,905. 68,983 ÷ 81,905 = 0.842. [MIDDLE_FIFTH.md](../06-simulation/median-lifestyle/MIDDLE_FIFTH.md) has 0.847 for 2023. If every household spent the middle amount, the total would fall by 15.8%. That is the 27.7% cut at the top, less 11.9% to bring the two lowest fifths up to the middle (862.2 + 484.2 = 1,346.4 billion dollars). **The 27.7% leaves the two lowest fifths where they are.**

## How to turn these into a share of hours

**Money spent is not hours worked.** Five steps stand between a spending share and a share of hours.

1. **Hours per dollar differ by product.** The top fifth makes 60.6% of education spending, 55.9% of spending on hotels and other lodging, 51.0% of spending on air fares and other public transport, and 31.5% of spending on food at home (Table 1101). Each product takes a different number of hours per dollar. The project's method in [MIDDLE_FIFTH.md](../06-simulation/median-lifestyle/MIDDLE_FIFTH.md) applies a ratio for each of 12 spending divisions to the hours behind that division. The same method works for the fourth and highest fifths. It needs their spending by division from Table 1101, which is in hand.
2. **Take out what is not consumption.** Pension and Social Security payments are 1,320.8 billion dollars. Cash gifts are 308.8 billion dollars. Neither buys hours of work in the year.
3. **Add what the survey misses.** The survey finds 75% to 78% of the national-accounts total for the items both count. Rich households answer surveys less often and report less. The missing spending is mostly at the top, so the true cut is larger than the survey shows. By how much is not measured.
4. **Count people, not households.** The hours figures are per person. The per-person cut is 20.3%, against 27.7% per household.
5. **Use one denominator.** The hours behind US consumption include hours worked abroad: 47% of them, by MIDDLE_FIFTH.md. A share of those hours is not a share of the US labour force.

**Worked example, with the limits stated.** MIDDLE_FIFTH.md puts the US average at 1,283 hours per person a year. If hours fell in step with per-person spending, a 20.3% cut would be 1,283 × 0.203 = **260 hours**. With pension payments left out it would be 1,283 × 0.168 = **216 hours**. Step 1 has not been done, so these are placeholders and not results.

**For the armed forces.** The rows give people and jobs. Hours need three more things.

1. **Hours per job.** Active-duty staff are full-time. A reservist is part-time, and no source for reserve days was opened.
2. **Hours abroad.** Imported parts and fuel took hours in other countries. Jobs counted in the United States leave them out.
3. **No double count.** Peltier's 5 jobs for each 1 million dollars already includes the uniformed and civilian staff. Do not add the head counts to it.

**A check from money.** Peltier gives 5 jobs for each 1 million dollars of military spending and 13 for education. Military work pays more per job, so a share of money overstates the share of hours. World military spending is 2.5% of world output, and uniformed staff are 0.79% of the world labour force. The share of hours lies between these two.

## Why this is a choice and not a saving

- **Cutting armed forces changes the level of defence.** The same defence does not get cheaper. A society that stood down half its forces would have half the forces.
- **Cutting consumption at the top changes how those households live.** The middle fifth's life costs the same hours as before. The cut frees hours only if the top two fifths accept the middle fifth's standard.
- **Class A and class B are different.** Thrown-away food and billing work can go while every household lives as it did. Class C cannot. [Data_sector-waste.md](Data_sector-waste.md) keeps the three classes apart for this reason, and the run must not add class C to the other two.
- **The freed hours are not automatically useful.** A shipyard worker or a private chef cannot grow food the next day. The run holds wants fixed and does not model retraining.
- **Part of the top's spending is not for themselves.** The highest fifth makes 60.6% of education spending. Much of it is college for their children.

## Limits

1. **The survey misses much spending by the very rich.** The CE has no top 1% group. Its top tenth averages $384,045 of income. Private jets, yachts and staff are barely in it. The SCF note says its sample of wealthy families adds about 0.5 trillion dollars of spending that a plain sample misses.
2. **The four sources for the top 10% do not agree.** 23.6% (CE), 25.7% (BLS experimental), 28% (SCF) and 49.7% (Moody's). The Minneapolis Fed review says the Moody's method is not a direct measure of spending. The Federal Reserve note also puts its own 28% far below the press figure. **Use 23.6% to 28% as the measured range, and treat 49.7% as an outer bound.**
3. **Income rank is not spending rank.** A retired couple with low income and high savings sits in a low fifth and spends like a high one. The lowest fifth spends $37,935 on an income of $18,125.
4. **A year of spending is not a year of consumption.** A car bought this year is used for 10 years. A house bought 20 years ago is consumed now. Mortgage principal is not in the survey's spending.
5. **Government services are not in the survey.** Schooling, roads and public health care are consumed by every fifth. MIDDLE_FIFTH.md holds them equal across fifths.
6. **The armed forces rows mix years.** People are for 2025, industry jobs for 2021 and 2024, spending for 2024, and the World Bank share for 2020.
7. **The defence industry has no official count.** The low and high values come from industry bodies and from one researcher's model. No BLS or Bureau of Economic Analysis count of defence-supported jobs was found. The Defense Manpower Data Center report was not opened; the personnel figures are from a magazine's reprint.
8. **Veterans' care, homeland security and nuclear weapons work at the Department of Energy are not in the US head counts.** Peltier counts 2,846,077 federal workers in defence, veterans' affairs and homeland security together in 2024.
9. **Induced jobs are not counted.** These are jobs supported when defence workers spend their pay. They would exist under any other use of the same hours.
10. **The carbon rows are for the world, not the United States.** A person in the world's top 10% is not always in the top 10% of the United States, so these rows do not check the US spending rows directly. A search summary of Chancel's paper says most of the top 1%'s emissions come from their investments and not from their consumption. That sentence was not read in the paper.
11. **The World Bank world figure stops at 2020.** It does not show the growth in forces since 2022.
12. **Sources that were looked for and not found.** A top 1% consumption share in Fisher, Johnson, Smeeding and Thompson: the [paper's page](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/inequality-in-3-d-income-consumption-and-wealth.htm) was opened and gives none. The Federal Reserve's Distributional Financial Accounts were not consulted; they cover wealth and not spending. No material-footprint figure by income was found.
