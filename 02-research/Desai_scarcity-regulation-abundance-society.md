## Why this matters to Aequitas

Aequitas has no patents and no copyright that earns ([Foundations](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations.md) §4.5). This essay is a mainstream legal source that says the same law exists to make copies scarce, and that creative output grew while that law went largely unenforced. It also says, plainly, which scarcity is real. That second part limits the claim that scarcity today is largely artificial.

## Key findings

**(a) What happens to goods whose copies cost almost nothing**

- The essay starts from standard economics. Competition pushes a price toward marginal cost, which is the cost of making one more unit. For information the cost of one more copy is close to zero. Economists therefore expected too little to be made, because a maker could not recover the cost of the first copy.
- The Internet separated making a work from distributing it. Distribution became close to free. The essay says output then rose in every field it looks at.
- Music. Four or five record companies once held nearly 80% of the market. Recording revenue fell from its 1999 peak until about 2011–2013. Global revenue then rose 7 years in a row from 2014 and reached $25.9 billion in 2021, up 18.5% on 2020.
- Video. Uploads to YouTube grew from 300 to more than 500 hours per minute.
- Books. Copies sold rose from 1 billion in 1993 to 2.3 billion in 2007. New titles rose from over 70,000 in 2002 to nearly 300,000 in 2012. With self-published and print-on-demand titles the count passed 3 million in 2010. Print was still 76% of 2021 sales revenue.
- The whole entertainment industry grew from $449 billion in 1998 to $745 billion in 2010 (the essay cites Travis 2015, *AIPLA Quarterly Journal* 43; no link found).
- Home video is the early case. Studios first sold tapes at $80–$90, and some later sold at $19.95. By 1996 rentals were $9.2 billion and sales $7.2 billion.
- The essay says physical goods come next, because a design can now be sent as a file and made on site. Its cases: a car from Local Motors with 49 parts, mostly 3D printed, whose design came from a public contest with a $7,500 prize; a father and son who built a copy of a Lamborghini at home for about $20,000; a home gene-editing kit sold for $120. Footnote 5 admits that Local Motors later changed business and failed.
- Its forecast is modest. Paid professionals and unpaid amateurs will both keep creating. Intellectual property law will stay and will matter less.

**(b) What law does to bring scarcity back, and the evidence that creation continues without it**

- Intellectual property. The essay says this law makes a good scarce on purpose. A copy takes nothing from anyone else, and the law restricts copying so that the maker can charge.
- It says enforcement failed. Copyright owners filed 50,000 lawsuits, won new laws, and ran school campaigns. Copying went on.
- Evidence that creation continued: more music releases and new artists, with quality at least as high ([Waldfogel 2012](https://doi.org/10.1086/665824); [Lunney 2012](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.2158874)); more book titles ([Waldfogel and Reimers 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.infoecopol.2015.02.001)); the file-sharing study of [Oberholzer-Gee and Strumpf 2010](https://doi.org/10.1086/605852); and unpaid shared production such as Wikipedia and open-source software. (DOIs are as printed in the source's reference list. They were not opened.)
- Entry rules. Taxi commissions limited the number of cabs and fixed fares. The essay calls this artificial scarcity, citing [Lemley and McKenna 2020, "Unfair Disruption"](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3344605) (DOI as printed, not opened).
- Utility rules. California had 1.3 million solar rooftops making about 10,000 megawatts by 2022. In December 2021 the state proposed to cut the rate paid to home producers by about 80% and to add a grid charge of about $60 a month. The essay reads this mainly as utilities defending central control. It also grants that the grid needs investment.
- Trademark and brands. A brand makes two identical goods differ in the buyer's mind. The essay gives premiums of 20% to 30% for a requested brand name, and up to 70% to 80% for brand-name medicines sold without prescription against identical generic ones. It reports the view that trademark law for luxury brands acts as a modern sumptuary code, meaning a rule about who may display what.
- Tokens. A non-fungible token (NFT) is a record that names one buyer as the owner of a digital file that anyone can still copy. The token for the first tweet sold for $2.9 million. At resale the best offer was $3,600 against an asking price of $48 million.
- Carmakers. Honda sent legal letters to sites that shared files for 3D-printing Honda parts.

**(c) What scarcity the essay says remains**

- Raw materials, energy, feedstock for printers, data centres and networks. It says outright that some things cannot be digitised.
- Demand that grows to meet supply. Cheap computing brought cryptocurrency mining and artificial-intelligence training, which use a great deal of electricity and hardware.
- Energy. The essay names power lines as the bottleneck. Lines are not built because of local opposition and disputes over rights of way. One Wyoming wind farm able to serve nearly 2 million customers cannot connect to the Southwest. It calls home nuclear power science fiction.
- Food. It says the world grew enough calories in 2012 for everyone plus 1.6 billion more people (its source is a [Johns Hopkins teaching primer](https://www.foodsystemprimer.org/food-production/history-of-agriculture/), URL as printed, not opened). It also reports about 770 million people undernourished and 2.37 billion without secure food in 2020 ([FAO Statistical Yearbook 2021](https://doi.org/10.4060/cb4477en), DOI as printed, not opened). Its one-line verdict on food: "Scarcity is social, economic, and political."
- Attention. More content means less attention for each item.
- Income. If machines do the work, people without wages cannot buy even cheap goods.

**(d) Who pays creators**

- The essay has no single answer. It lists what it sees: sales of a physical form (bound books), live events (concerts), subscriptions and advertising on platforms, prizes, and unpaid work by amateurs.
- Cory Doctorow is the named case. He gives his novels away as files and sells printed copies. His stated aim is to be found by readers.
- For people in general it turns to public policy: a tax on robots, a universal basic income (a regular payment to every person), the Danish system of unemployment insurance with retraining, and public retraining schemes. It favours a basic income and says the detail is outside the essay.
- It says the United States tax system favours corporations over individuals and capital over labour, and that this should be reversed.

## What we can use

- **A citable statement of what intellectual property law is for.** Two senior law professors say the law exists to make a copy scarce so that it can be sold above its cost. That matches [Foundations](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations.md) §4.5 (the rule that there are no patents) and [cost-not-price](../01-wiki/cost-not-price.md). Cite it for the diagnosis only.
- **The count evidence that creation did not stop.** Titles from 70,000 (2002) to nearly 300,000 (2012); uploads from 300 to 500 hours per minute. Use it against the objection that nobody creates without exclusive rights. See the limits below before using it.
- **Doctorow and the first-time creator.** [Foundations](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations.md) §4.5 admits that a first-time creator attracts no pledges and must first make small work and gather feedback. Doctorow's practice is the same path in today's economy: give the work away, be found, get paid later.
- **Three worked cases of law-made scarcity with numbers.** Taxi commissions; the California proposal (about 80% cut, about $60 a month); brand premiums of 20% to 80% on identical goods. These fit the table in [Foundations](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations.md) §4.6 that says much scarcity is produced, and [pledge-and-signal](../01-wiki/pledge-and-signal.md).
- **Farm labour as a caution on the project's own hours figure.** United States farm work fell from 41% of workers in 1900 to under 2% in 2002. The project's ratio of 0.63 (world hours against a typical United States life, §3.5) is measured at today's methods. This history shows the hours a good needs can fall a long way. It is a reason to label 0.63 as a present-method figure, and no evidence that the gap is closed.
- **The essay's own comparison with Star Trek.** It says the replicator future is far off for physical goods. That agrees with the citation rule in [post-scarcity](../01-wiki/post-scarcity.md).

## What it gets wrong / limitations

- **Its evidence is not from a world without copyright.** Copyright law stood in full through 1999–2021. What weakened was enforcement against individual copiers. The essay shows that creation continued under weak enforcement. It does not show what happens with no such law.
- **The music recovery was paid through copyright.** The rise to $25.9 billion came from licensed streaming subscriptions. That is income from exclusive rights under a new business model. It is weak support for Aequitas, where nothing is charged per copy.
- **It counts works, and never counts hours or livelihoods.** More titles and more uploaded hours do not show that more people could live from creative work. The essay itself worries that paid professional work may shrink. Aequitas credits hours, so this is the figure that would matter, and the essay does not have it.
- **It has no mechanism for paying creators.** Aequitas pledges are hours committed to named makers, before or after the work. The essay's nearest things are prizes ($7,500 for a car design) and a basic income paid from tax. Both sit inside a money economy with profit, and Aequitas has no tax ([no-taxation](../01-wiki/no-taxation.md)).
- **The cases of abundant physical goods are thin.** One car maker that failed, one home-built car, one $120 kit. None of them is a measurement of cost.
- **It reports the studies that favour its case.** I saw no study cited for the opposite finding on file sharing. I read through an extraction tool and may have missed one.
- **One figure looks wrong.** The essay says 54% of German energy consumption comes from renewable sources. Figures of that size are normally given for electricity, which is a part of energy use. I did not check the essay's cited news source. Do not quote the 54%.
- **Its food claim is one number from a teaching primer.** Calories grown in 2012 say nothing about protein, cost of delivery, or the hours of work behind them.
- **The authors are advocates.** Lemley has argued this position since at least 2015. The essay is an introduction to a collection the same two authors edited.

## Against the claim that scarcity is artificial

The claim under test (2026-11-19): scarcity today is largely artificial; much is held by a few; laws are written that make the concentration of wealth worse.

**What the essay supports**

- Law does create scarcity in copies, in taxi rides, and in branded goods. This part of the claim has direct support.
- It says the tax system favours capital over labour, and that over about 40 years nearly all the gains from cheaper goods and higher productivity went to the rich and to capital. This supports the third part of the claim, as an assertion. The essay cites [Piketty, *Capital in the Twenty-First Century*](https://doi.org/10.4159/9780674369542-014) (DOI as printed, not opened) and gives no figures of its own.
- On food it says enough is grown and that hunger has social, economic and political causes.

**What the essay does not address**

- Housing and land. They are not discussed. The essay gives no support for a claim that housing is plentiful.
- How much wealth a few people hold. It has no distribution figures.
- Whether the world's working hours are enough. It has no measurement to set beside the project's 0.63.

**What limits or contradicts the claim**

- **The essay is about goods with near-zero copy cost. It does not show that food, housing or energy are plentiful.** On food it asserts enough calories from one figure for one year. On energy it says supply is still constrained. On housing it says nothing.
- It states that raw materials, energy and infrastructure stay scarce for physical reasons, and that some things cannot be digitised.
- It says cheap supply creates new demand that uses the supply up. Its examples are cryptocurrency mining and artificial-intelligence computing.
- The causes of hunger it names are war, extreme weather, the pandemic and broken supply lines. Those are not the same as supply held back by a few owners.
- The energy bottleneck it names is power lines blocked by many local objectors. That is a political cause, and it is not concentration of ownership.
- It grants that the utilities have a real cost on their side: the grid must be kept up, and the cost falls on the customers who remain.
- It says intellectual property will stay and is a needed incentive for some creative work. For medicines and regulated biotechnology it says makers need some way to recover their outlay.
- Part of artificial scarcity is wanted by buyers. People pay for luxury brands and tokens to show status. The essay says only that there is some reason to think markets create scarcity in this way, and it calls the future of such goods an open question. See [Veblen](Veblen_conspicuous-consumption.md).
- Ending a law-made scarcity did not spread the gain. When ride-sharing ended the taxi limits, riders got cheaper trips, and drivers got doubts over pay and health cover. The essay says abundance so far has produced new winners and deeper inequality. So removing artificial scarcity is not enough by itself to stop concentration.
- Attention is finite. More works compete for the same hours of a person's day.
- The authors call a society of general plenty a distant prospect.
