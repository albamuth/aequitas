# Who Watches the Watchers — the "Whoever Pays Gets a Favourable Verdict" Problem

**Version:** 0.2
**Type:** empirical / institutional record — used as a **counter-argument** against an Aequitas proposal
**Subjects:** Arthur Andersen & Enron (2001–02); the credit-rating agencies and the 2007–08 financial crisis; the Sarbanes–Oxley Act (2002)

## Why this matters to Aequitas

**This source killed a claim the project was about to lean on** — which is the most useful thing a source can do.

Aequitas relies on independent groups (call them "trust networks") to check people's claims and audit the cost figures. An early version of that idea rested on a comforting assumption: *these networks will compete on accuracy, because accurate checks are what their members want.* The historical record says the opposite, repeatedly and expensively.

## The finding

> **The party paying for a verdict doesn't want an *accurate* verdict — they want a *favourable* one. Accuracy benefits the other side.**

Whenever the party being judged pays for the judging, the judgements drift in their favour. This isn't about individual dishonesty; it's about which way the money points.

**Two well-documented cases:**

- **Auditing.** The accounting firm Arthur Andersen was paid by Enron — and also sold Enron lucrative consulting on the side. When Enron collapsed in fraud, Andersen was convicted of obstruction (later overturned on a technicality) and collapsed anyway. The legislative response, the Sarbanes–Oxley Act, was *structural* — it restricted the extra services an auditor may sell the company it audits. That restriction is itself an admission that *competition alone doesn't discipline the relationship.*
- **Credit ratings.** Under the "the issuer pays" model, rating agencies were paid by the very banks whose products they rated. They stamped top-grade ratings on securities that later defaulted en masse. The official [crisis inquiry](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-FCIC/pdf/GPO-FCIC.pdf) treated this ratings failure as a *central* cause of the crash, not a footnote.

## The correction it forced

**A proposed Aequitas defence was rejected on this evidence.** The proposal was: *trust networks are member-owned co-ops funded by member dues, so with no profit motive they have no reason to go easy on anyone.*

> **The conflict is about direction, not profit.** Making Arthur Andersen a co-op *owned by its clients* would have been worse than the arrangement Enron actually paid for, not better. Removing the profit motive leaves the go-easy motive completely intact.

**What Aequitas genuinely does remove, and should claim:** there's no profit-rich giant corporation able to fund convenient science, because Aequitas removes profit from exchange in the first place ([Foundations §A5](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.28.md)). **The funding channel for corruption closes. The directional pull does not.**

## What was adopted instead

Two structural rules, neither needing an enforcer — see [rival-sector-audit](../01-wiki/rival-sector-audit.md) and [Foundations §3.3a](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.28.md):

1. **A trust network made up mostly of the people it's supposed to be auditing is compromised by its own composition.** In Aequitas everyone is both a producer and a consumer, so a *general-membership* network is dominated, for any given product, by the people who *consume* it — whose interests point the right way automatically. And because **who belongs to a network is public**, this is something anyone can *check*, not a rule someone has to enforce.
2. **Rival-sector auditing** — because rule 1 creates its own slant. A network of consumers wants the recorded cost of what they *buy* to read *low*, and nobody funds correcting an error in their own favour. But a *rival producer* does: if beef's cost is understated, plant-protein makers are directly harmed and will fund the correction. **Consumers police one direction; rivals police both.**

**Read the two rules together — neither is safe alone.** This is the project's open "understatement drift" problem: errors that make something look *cheaper* than it is have nobody obviously funding their correction. See [Foundations §10](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.28.md).

## What this source does *not* settle

- **Whether rival-sector auditing actually works.** No historical example of it was found — it's a proposal, not a documented mechanism. **Needs a simulation.**
- **Whether reputation ever disciplines the "issuer pays" arrangement.** The "subscriber pays" model existed in credit ratings before the 1970s; why it was displaced, and whether it was more accurate, **isn't researched here and should be.**
- **The legal status of the Andersen conviction.** It was overturned; the firm died regardless. Don't overstate the verdict — the useful fact is the *structural* response (the new law), not the court ruling.
