## Why this matters to Aequitas

This essay supplies the historical backing for one of the project's core claims: **a market and capitalism are not the same thing.** A market is many small sellers and buyers meeting, with prices settling from the bottom up. Capitalism, in this reading, is the *opposite* — a handful of large players big enough to set prices instead of taking them.

That distinction is the answer to a question Aequitas will be asked constantly: *"So you want to abolish markets?"* No. Aequitas keeps ordinary buying and selling. What it dissolves are the entities that were never really in the market in the first place — the ones large enough to rig it.

This connects to three things the reader will meet elsewhere:
- **Price equals cost.** In Aequitas a price is not a number someone chooses in order to make a profit; it is simply a statement of what a thing physically took to make. See [Foundations A5 (cost, not price)](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.38.md#a5-cost-not-price).
- **Why capitalism can't operate inside the system.** Because there is no profit to skim and nothing to hoard, the machinery of accumulation has nothing to grab. See [Foundations §7.1 (Capitalism cannot function)](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.38.md#71-capitalism-cannot-function).
- **The whole public framing** of what Aequitas is *against* — captured concentration, not commerce.

## Key findings

- Braudel describes **two layers, not one.** At the bottom: market towns full of small producers, where price is set from below by competition. Above them: long-distance trade and finance controlled by a few big operators — this upper layer is what he calls "capitalism," and it behaves more like an *anti*-market.
- DeLanda's summary of it: capitalism *"has always engaged in anti-competitive practices, manipulating demand and supply in a variety of ways."*
- Inside a large firm, he notes, *"prices are increasingly replaced by commands as coordinating mechanisms."* Put plainly: a giant corporation does not run on internal price signals — it runs on orders from managers. By that measure it is not really a market participant at all.
- *"There is a dialectic still very much alive between capitalism on one hand, and its antithesis, the 'non-capitalism' of the lower level."* (The two layers are in permanent tension.)
- On where innovation actually comes from: *"It was not the capitalists who brought about the first cotton revolution; all the new ideas came from enterprising small businesses."*
- *"Markets, ecosystems and decentralized networks have in common… synergistic properties [that] emerge spontaneously"* — useful order arising with nobody in charge of it.
- He cites the economist **John Kenneth Galbraith** for the claim that roughly **half of all US organisations** would count as anti-market institutions, connected to real supply and demand only indirectly. *(This is a second-hand attribution — check it against Galbraith's own writing before quoting it.)*
- The market/anti-market split cuts against **both** the free-market right and the Marxist left, each of which treats "capitalism" as one unified system rather than two layers.

## What we can use

- **A framing line for the introduction:** Aequitas is pro-market and anti-capitalist, and here are five centuries of economic history saying those two positions are perfectly compatible.
- **A clean, non-ideological definition of the target.** The people who *set* prices because they are big enough to (as against the people who *take* whatever price the market offers) are exactly the entities Aequitas dissolves. This is handy for a civic-reformer audience, for whom the bare word "anti-capitalist" is a liability.
- **Meshwork versus hierarchy.** DeLanda's picture of decentralised networks generating order on their own maps directly onto two of Aequitas's design goals: that it can be checked by anyone without a central authority, and that it should sustain and spread on its own.

## What it gets wrong / limitations

- **It is purely diagnostic — it offers no mechanism.** DeLanda says how prices *shouldn't* form (neither by monopoly nor by managerial command) but nothing about how they *should*. Aequitas has to supply that entire answer itself; this essay contributes none of the machinery.
- The market/anti-market split is a **tendency, not a clean taxonomy**, and economic historians argue about it. Braudel's own model actually has *three* tiers (everyday material life / the market economy / capitalism), which is more careful than DeLanda's compression into two.
- DeLanda writes in a dense philosophical style (borrowed from Gilles Deleuze) that will not survive contact with a professional economics audience. **For any academic write-up, cite Braudel directly**; save DeLanda for the popular text.
- Risk of over-claiming. Real small-producer markets are themselves full of unequal information and local monopolies. "Markets are fine, capitalism is the problem" is tidier than the messy truth.
