## How to turn these into a share of hours

### Clothing

**The row to use is "destroyed before use": 4% low, 9% high.** The "not sold" rows (10% to 21%) are not waste rows. Most unsold clothing is later sold cheaply, exported or given away, and somebody wears it.

The arithmetic: wasted hours = hours of clothing in a typical life × share destroyed.

**Worked example.** Suppose clothing takes 40 of the 1,380 hours. The 40 is made up for this example. The run must take the real figure from the median-lifestyle tracks.

- Low: 40 × 0.04 = 1.6 hours a year.
- High: 40 × 0.09 = 3.6 hours a year.
- As a share of the whole life: 1.6 ÷ 1,380 = 0.12%. 3.6 ÷ 1,380 = 0.26%.

**A stricter low: 2%.** Germany's 10% unsold × the EEA's one fifth destroyed = 2%. This joins two sources that were not made to be joined. Use it only as a test of how much the result depends on the low value.

**Assumptions, each one a weakness:**

1. A tonne destroyed took the same hours as a tonne sold. Returned goods also took shipping and sorting hours, so this is on the low side.
2. The European share holds for the United States. No US figure was found.
3. The 1,380 hours already contain the hours for destroyed goods. This holds if the hours come from total industry output. Check it in the method.
4. Recycled goods count as destroyed. Recycling gets back fibre. It does not get back the sewing hours.
5. The 4% and 9% count returns and unsold stock from sources that overlap. The EEA says so.

### Other consumer goods

France is the only place with a figure across all non-food goods. Unsold goods are 3.0% of sales. 10% of those are neither sold off, recycled nor given away.

- Low: 0.03 × 0.10 = 0.3% of the sector's output.
- High, counting recycled goods as lost: 0.03 × 0.37 = 1.1%.

**Worked example.** If non-food goods other than clothing took 150 of the 1,380 hours (made up), the waste is 150 × 0.003 = 0.45 hours low and 150 × 0.011 = 1.65 hours high.

### Housing

**An empty home is a stock. The 1,380 hours are a yearly flow.** The hours that built an empty house are already spent. Nobody gets them back. What can be saved is building and upkeep in the years ahead.

**How the low and the high were built** (United States, 2024, thousands of units):

| Step | Low | High |
|---|--:|--:|
| Start: "other" vacant units | 3,526 | 3,526 |
| Reasons counted as "empty and usable" | Personal or family reasons 29.0% + storage 5.3% = 34.3% | The low, plus needs repairs 16.1%, legal proceedings 4.5%, foreclosure 1.2%, other or not known 7.0% = 63.1% |
| Units | 3,526 × 0.343 = 1,209 | 3,526 × 0.631 = 2,225 |
| Share of all 146,770 units | **0.8%** | **1.5%** |

Left out of both: units for rent, for sale, or waiting for a household (normal turnover). Units being repaired or made ready for sale (already on their way back). Long absence and special use (somebody uses them). Abandoned or condemned (not usable). Seasonal and occasional homes (class C, below).

**Worked example.** Suppose building and keeping up homes takes 100 of the 1,380 hours (made up). If the same people were housed in a stock 0.8% smaller, the saving is 100 × 0.008 = 0.8 hours a year. At 1.5% it is 1.5 hours a year. As a share of the whole life: 0.06% to 0.11%.

**A case for 0.** NAHB says the market has 1.2 million fewer vacant units than its long-run norm. The low above is 1.2 million units. A critic can say the two cancel and the usable excess is 0. The run should report the housing row at 0 as well.

**Second homes are a separate row, class C.** Seasonal 3,455 + occasional 2,008 + occupied by people who live elsewhere 1,079 = 6,542 thousand units, or 4.5% of the stock. Somebody uses these homes. Giving them up changes the standard of living. With the high row, the ceiling is 1.5% + 4.5% = 6.0% of the stock.

**Germany.** 2.5% of dwellings had been empty for 12 months or more. 1.7% were ready to live in within 3 months. The census does not cross these two, so there is no figure for "long empty and ready". Much of the long-term vacancy is in regions that are losing people.

### Offices

About 18% to 20% of US office space is vacant. No source gave a normal rate, so the excess over turnover is not known. The building hours are spent. Do not use this row until the run has a figure for office building and upkeep hours and a normal vacancy rate.

### Farm surpluses and advertising mail

- **US Section 32 purchases are not waste in class A.** The food is eaten. It is 791 to 871 million dollars a year with no tonnage. Run it at 0.
- **Wine distillation** is real destruction of a product: 1.2 million hectolitres in 2026 and 3.0 to 4.4 million in 2023. The alcohol is used for disinfectant and fuel. I did not retrieve French wine output, so there is no share.
- **Advertising mail** is 52.2% of US mail pieces. It belongs in class B (advertising), and it is already inside the postal and advertising hours. Do not count it again in class A.

## What cannot be cut

- **Normal turnover in housing.** People can move only if some homes stand empty. In 2024 units for rent, for sale, or waiting for a household were 5,031 thousand, or 3.4% of the stock. The rental rate (5.7%) was below its long-run mean (6.6%), so none of this is excess.
- **Homes under repair.** 15.2% of "other" vacant units were being repaired and 7.9% were being made ready to rent or sell.
- **Seasonal homes that someone uses.** A cabin used 6 weeks a year is used. Cutting it is a class C choice.
- **Homes in the wrong place.** An empty house in a shrinking town does not house a person in a city with jobs. This applies to the German long-term figure most of all.
- **Safety stock.** A shop that never runs out of a size must hold more than it sells. Unsold goods that are later sold cheaply are stock that did its job late.
- **Returns that go back on sale.** In Germany 93.2% of returned items were sold again as new.
- **Goods that cannot be sold.** Damaged, recalled or unsafe goods must be destroyed. The EU ban on destroying unsold clothing has exemptions for such cases.
- **Food bought as surplus and then eaten.** Section 32 food is in this group.

## Limits

- **No US figure for destroyed clothing or destroyed returns exists in what I found.** The clothing rows are European.
- **The EEA figures rest on company self-reports and press reports.** The EEA says the true share is probably higher. The EU now requires large firms to disclose what they destroy. That data will replace these rows.
- **I could not read any PDF.** So the ETC/CE report (the 5 values behind 21%, and how 4% to 9% was built), the Ellen MacArthur Foundation report, the HUD reports and the BBSR analysis are unread. Searches of the coverage of the Ellen MacArthur report found no "never sold" share in it. From memory it has a small figure for garments that never reach the market. That is a lead to check and not a number to use.
- **The housing low and high are my own sorting of the Census reasons.** Another sorter would get other values. "Personal or family reasons" (29.0%) decides the low, and the survey does not say what those reasons are.
- **The two US surveys disagree.** "Other" vacant is 3,526 thousand in the Housing Vacancy Survey and 4,916 thousand in the American Community Survey. With the second figure the low and high become 1.1% and 2.1%.
- **The 2025 Housing Vacancy Survey figures exist** (148,163 thousand units, "other" vacant 3,386) and rest on 11 months, because October 2025 was not collected. I used 2024.
- **No European Union figure for empty dwellings was found.** Eurostat's census data has the category. I did not reach the values.
- **Packaging: nothing found.** I did not search it in depth. Run it at 0.
- **Farm surplus: no tonnage found** for US purchases, and no figure for EU fruit and vegetable withdrawals or for dumped milk.
- **Tonnes, euros and units are not hours.** Every worked example above uses made-up hours for the sector. The run must put in the real ones.
- **No page asked the reader to do anything.** No instruction aimed at a reader or a program was seen in any page opened.
