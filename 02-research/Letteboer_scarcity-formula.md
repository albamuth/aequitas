## Why this matters to Aequitas

Foundations §4.6 says that much scarcity is produced. This paper tries to turn that sentence into a test with three conditions, and it says what evidence would make each condition fail. That is useful because it gives a way to sort one shortage from another. It is also a limit on the author's claim of 2026-11-19, because the test itself admits that some scarcity is natural and that some has no one behind it.

## Key findings

- **The thesis.** Scarcity is not mainly a natural condition. It is mainly the result of how institutions are designed. The abstract concludes that abundance is then a matter of design too. *(Source: SSRN abstract.)*
- **A three-part test for "artificial scarcity".** A scarcity counts as artificial when all three hold: (1) there is demonstrated technical capacity to supply plenty; (2) access is restricted by a rule that someone enforces, by law, by contract or by regulation; (3) there is a named actor who gains, as a matter of structure, from the scarcity continuing. *(Source: SSRN abstract.)*
- **Each condition has a stated way to fail.** *(Source: fragments of the Dutch version, section 1.1, "Kunstmatige schaarste".)*
  - Condition 1 fails if an independent technical analysis shows the scarcity comes from physical or ecological limits that cannot be overcome, even with the best institutions and a reasonable level of investment. The paper then calls the scarcity primarily natural and puts it outside the test.
  - Condition 2 fails if the scarcity comes from a failure to coordinate, a lack of information, or a collective-action problem, with no beneficiary who keeps the restriction in place. The paper then says the restriction is not strategic.
  - Condition 3 fails if nobody can be shown to gain as a matter of structure, or if the gain is a diffuse side effect with nothing enforcing it.
- **Technical capacity is necessary and not sufficient.** Capacity alone does not make a scarcity artificial. The other two conditions must hold as well. *(Source: Dutch fragments.)*
- **A weaker grade exists.** "Probable artificial scarcity" means 2 of the 3 conditions are documented and the third is plausible and not fully proven. *(Source: Dutch fragments.)*
- **Fourteen domains are examined.** As listed from the abstract by the search index: finance, energy, healthcare, food, housing, water, education, media, intellectual property, cloud computing, digital infrastructure, production, cognition and governance. I could not check this list against the paper.
- **Cases seen in the fragments.** Digital copies restricted by copyright, paywalls and platform monopolies. Farming restricted by concentrated land ownership, dependence on bought inputs, and intellectual property on seed. Finance, where the money system and concentrated wealth are named as a basic mechanism of scarcity. Manufactured goods, where planned obsolescence (designing a product to fail or date early), blocks on repair, and closed product ecosystems are named. Education, media and creative work, where cost, debt and long lead times are named. Patent thickets in pharmaceuticals (many overlapping patents around one drug).
- **The author's own statement of strength.** The paper presents its reading as having strong explanatory power and says it is not proven beyond doubt. It cites Ragin (2014) and Gerring (2017) at that point; I did not see the titles. Those are standard names in case-study method, so the evidence appears to be comparison of described cases.
- **Reception.** The SSRN author page shows this as the author's only paper, with 24 downloads and no recorded citations at the time the search index read it. I found no review of it and no response to it.
- **Numbers.** I saw no measured totals: no hours, no tonnes, no share of output. I cannot say whether the 341 pages contain any.

## What we can use

- **A checkable definition for §4.6.** Foundations §4.6 states that supply is held back to keep a number up. The three conditions, with their failure cases, give that statement a form that can be tested on one good at a time: is there capacity, is there an enforced restriction, is there a beneficiary.
- **A sorting rule for the tolerance in §5.5.3.** A network sets its tolerance low in a real shortage. Condition 1's failure case is a usable description of "real": physical or ecological limits that remain under good institutions and reasonable investment. A shortage that passes all three conditions calls for removing the restriction, and lowering the tolerance would ration the wrong thing.
- **Cases for §4.5 (no patents).** Patent thickets, seed intellectual property and copyright on digital copies are examples of scarcity that exists only because copying is forbidden. These fit the Foundations position that ideas replicate freely.
- **Support for part of the author's claim.** The paper names planned obsolescence and repair blocks as scarcity that is designed. It names concentrated wealth and land. Condition 3 is the "a few benefit" part of the claim, stated as something to be shown case by case.
- **The category the claim leaves out.** Condition 2's failure case describes scarcity that is neither natural nor engineered by anyone: it comes from poor coordination or missing information. Aequitas should say which of its mechanisms address that kind. Pledges (Foundations §4.6) are a coordination signal, so this is the kind the project has the most to say about.
- **Cite it as a framework, not as evidence.** It is safe to cite for the three-part definition. It is not safe to cite for any claim about how much scarcity is artificial.

## What it gets wrong / limitations

- **Not peer-reviewed.** It is a self-published working paper by an independent researcher with no institutional affiliation stated. One author covers 14 sectors in 341 pages, which suggests breadth over depth. I found no independent check of any case.
- **I did not read the full text.** Every point here rests on an abstract and on search fragments. A fragment can drop a qualifier. Treat each finding above as provisional until someone reads the document.
- **The test works one sector at a time.** "Demonstrated technical capacity" in housing, and again in energy, and again in healthcare, does not show that all of them can be supplied at once. They draw on the same working hours and the same energy. A sector-by-sector test cannot see that shared budget. Foundations §3.5 measures exactly that budget.
- **No measure of labour, as far as I could see.** The project's own result is that the world's working hours cover about 0.63 of a typical United States life for everyone, and 0.72 to 0.75 by the methods of Germany, Japan, France and Sweden. Nothing I read in this paper counts hours, so it neither confirms nor answers that result.
- **"Technical capacity" is not defined in what I read.** A demonstration at one plant, in one country, at one scale is not capacity for 8.1 billion people. I could not check how the paper handles scale.
- **"Reasonable investment" is a judgment.** Condition 1's failure case depends on what counts as a reasonable level of investment. Whoever applies the test sets that level. Foundations §3.5 warns against a check whose pass condition is set by the checker.
- **Selection of cases.** The paper chose 14 domains where it expected to find artificial scarcity. I saw no domain reported as a clear case of natural scarcity. A test that is only shown passing has not been shown able to fail.
- **Remedies were not read.** The SSRN keywords include commons governance and cooperative economics, so the paper probably proposes these. I could not read what it proposes or whether it tests the proposals.
- **It reasons about access and price, not cost.** From what I read, the paper treats scarcity as blocked access. Aequitas counts what a thing used up. The paper does not appear to ask what plenty would cost in hours, material and energy.

## Against the claim that scarcity is artificial

The claim under test (2026-11-19): scarcity today is largely artificial; productive capacity exceeds consumption; much is hoarded by a few; much is wasted on war, luxury, planned obsolescence, surplus dumping and needless cheap goods; people are conditioned to think there is not enough.

**What the source supports**

- Scarcity as mainly institutional: supported as a thesis and argued by described cases. Not measured.
- Hoarding by a few: partly supported. Concentrated wealth and land are named, and condition 3 requires a named beneficiary.
- Planned obsolescence: supported. It is named with repair blocks and closed ecosystems.
- Conditioning: possibly addressed. "Cognition" is one of the 14 domains. I could not read that part.

**What the source does not address, in what I read**

- War, luxury, surplus dumping and needless cheap goods. None appeared in the abstract or the fragments. I cannot say they are absent from 341 pages.
- Whether total productive capacity exceeds total consumption. The paper tests sectors. It gives no total.

**What in the source limits or contradicts the claim**

- **The paper says "not primarily natural", and builds in natural scarcity.** Condition 1's failure case concedes that some scarcity comes from physical or ecological limits that good institutions and reasonable investment do not remove. The paper puts such scarcity outside its test. So the paper does not claim all scarcity is artificial.
- **Some scarcity has no hoarder.** Condition 2's failure case concedes scarcity from coordination failure, missing information and collective-action problems. Nobody holds anything back in those cases. This contradicts the part of the claim that puts hoarding by a few at the centre.
- **A gain is not proof of design.** Condition 3's failure case says that a diffuse benefit with nothing enforcing it does not make a scarcity artificial. Someone profiting from a shortage is not enough.
- **The author admits the case is not proven.** The paper calls its reading explanatory and says it is not established beyond doubt. It also has a "probable" grade for cases where only 2 of 3 conditions are documented.
- **Capacity is necessary and not sufficient.** By the paper's own rule, showing that humanity could make enough of something does not show that its scarcity is artificial.
- **A rival view is recorded inside the paper.** A comparison in the Dutch version describes a position labelled "7-Stacks" that treats biophysical scarcity as a hard upper limit and does not separate natural from artificial. I could not tell what "7-Stacks" is or how the paper answers it.
- **Energy.** The paper lists energy as a domain of artificial scarcity. Foundations §3.5 finds energy the tightest physical limit, at 0.19 of what a median standard needs in the one-country case it ran. These point in opposite directions. I could not read the paper's energy chapter, so I cannot say which evidence it uses.

**Is the source one-sided?** In aim, yes: it sets out to find artificial scarcity and reports 14 domains where it does. In method, less so: its test states how it can fail, and those failure cases are the strongest material here against the author's claim. **Net: the paper supports "much scarcity is produced" as Foundations §4.6 already says it. It does not support "productive capacity exceeds consumption" and it does not touch the §3.5 hours measurement.**
