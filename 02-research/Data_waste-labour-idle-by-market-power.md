# Data: labour left idle by buyouts, mergers and employers' power over wages

**Version:** 0.1
**Type:** dataset note
**Retrieved:** 2026-11-19
**Read:** Full text of 5 papers and reports, read from the PDF: the US Treasury report "The State of Labor Market Competition" (March 2022); Berger, Herkenhoff and Mongey, "Labor Market Power" (NBER working paper 25719, revision of May 2021); Davis and others, "The (Heterogenous) Economic Effects of Private Equity Buyouts" (NBER working paper 26371, revision of April 2024); Arnold, "Mergers and Acquisitions, Local Labor Market Concentration, and Worker Outcomes" (draft of 1 September 2025); Lamadon, Mogstad and Setzler (NBER working paper 25954). The last column of each row says what was opened.
**Local copy:** none

This note is about the cause of idle labour. The counts of idle labour are in [Data: capacity left idle](Data_waste-idle-capacity.md) and are not repeated here.

**Four warnings before the rows.**

- **Most rows are about one firm against similar firms.** A job cut at a bought firm is not an idle person. The worker may have a new job a month later. No source in this note measures how many stay idle.
- **The two large numbers come from models.** The hours lost to employers' wage-setting power are a model result, not a count. The model has no unemployed people in it.
- **Papers change between versions.** The 2019 and 2024 versions of the main buyout paper give different figures. Each row names the version.
- **"Low" and "High" are the ends of what the sources allow.** They are not a best guess with a margin.

## The claim under test

> "Part of the labour force is idle because of how capitalism works. Private equity firms and large companies buy up businesses and merge them, cut staff, and so make every job highly competitive. That favours the owners, because they can then keep wages low."

The claim has 4 parts. Each can be checked alone.

1. Buyouts and mergers cut staff.
2. Employers have power over wages and use it.
3. That power leaves labour idle.
4. Consolidation has made this worse over time.

## Rows

Terms used in the rows:

- **PE** is private equity: a fund that buys a whole firm, mostly with borrowed money, to sell it later.
- **Control firms** are firms of the same industry, size and age that were not bought.
- **HHI** is the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index: the sum of the squared market shares of all employers in a local labour market. 0 is many small employers. 10,000 is 1 employer.
- **Markdown** is the gap between what a worker's work adds to the firm's revenue and the wage.

## How to turn these into hours

All counts are in thousands of people and come from [Data: capacity left idle](Data_waste-idle-capacity.md): 163,551 employed in 2025, a labour force of 170,833, a population of 341,900, and 1,750 hours a year per worker. The household survey gives 38.3 hours × 52 weeks = 1,992 hours a year, which is the upper figure.

**Row (a): hours lost to employers' wage-setting power.**

The model of Berger, Herkenhoff and Mongey compares the economy as it is with one where no employer can pay below what the work adds. Employment in the second economy is higher by 0.8%, 11.3% or 22.5%. The 3 values are for 3 settings of one dial: how much more people work when pay rises. The settings are 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8, the range that the paper says the CBO uses.

- LOW: 0.8% × 163,551 = 1,308 thousand people. × 1,750 hours = 2.3 billion hours a year. Per head of population: 2.3 billion ÷ 341.9 million = 7 hours.
- Middle: 11.3% × 163,551 = 18,481 thousand people. × 1,750 = 32.3 billion hours. Per head: 95 hours.
- HIGH: 22.5% × 163,551 = 36,799 thousand people. × 1,750 = 64.4 billion hours. Per head: 188 hours.

The United States works 837 hours per head at 1,750 hours per worker. Row (a) lifts that to 844 at the low end and 1,025 at the high end. Against the 1,380 hours needed, that is 0.61 to 0.74.

As a share of the hours of the economy without wage-setting power: 0.8 ÷ 100.8 = 0.8%, and 22.5 ÷ 122.5 = 18.4%.

**Row (b): unemployment held above its lowest level.**

`people = (CBO rate − floor) × labour force`

- LOW: the floor is 3.4%, the lowest month since 2000. 4.4 − 3.4 = 1.0 point. 1.0% × 170,833 = 1,708 thousand people. 1,708 ÷ 163,551 = **1.0%** of hours worked. × 1,750 hours = 3.0 billion hours a year (3.4 billion at 1,992 hours). Per head: 9 to 10 hours.
- HIGH: the floor is 1.3%, the people out of work for under 5 weeks. 4.4 − 1.3 = 3.1 points. 3.1% × 170,833 = 5,296 thousand people. 5,296 ÷ 163,551 = **3.2%** of hours worked. × 1,750 hours = 9.3 billion hours a year (10.5 billion at 1,992 hours). Per head: 27 to 31 hours.
- A middle floor of 2.9%, the lowest yearly rate since 1948: 1.5 points, 2,562 thousand people, 1.6% of hours worked.

The unemployment rate in 2025 was 4.3%, a tenth of a point below the CBO rate. With 4.3 in place of 4.4 the results are 0.9% and 3.1%.

**The plan of September 2022, as a check.** The Federal Reserve projected a rise of 0.6 points while it raised rates. 0.6% × 170,833 = 1,025 thousand people, or 0.6% of hours worked. The rate did rise, from 3.4% in April 2023 to 4.3% in 2025. No source opened here shows how much of that rise the rate increases caused.

**Buyouts and mergers: jobs moved, not hours lost.** These figures are gross. They are shown so that nobody mistakes them for idle hours.

- Mergers: 2% of workers a year are in a workplace that changes owner. 2% × 163,551 = 3,271 thousand. Employment there falls 13.4% against matched workplaces. 3,271 × 13.4% = 438 thousand jobs a year, or 0.27% of employment.
- Buyouts: 11.7 to 13.3 million workers at PE-backed firms. At the all-buyout effect of −1.8%: 211 to 239 thousand jobs, or 0.13% to 0.15% of employment. The −1.8% is a 2-year change after a buyout and is not statistically different from 0. Putting it on the whole stock of PE-backed firms is a rough ceiling.

## What the evidence supports

- **Some kinds of buyout cut staff.** Employment falls 11.9% after a listed firm is taken private. Staff fall 11.6% at bought hospitals. Paid care hours fall 3% at bought nursing homes.
- **Buyouts lower pay.** Pay per worker falls 3.1% against control firms in 2 years.
- **Merged workplaces shrink.** Employment falls 13.4% against matched workplaces. In Belgium, mergers classed as done for market power cut 14% of jobs for good.
- **Mergers that raise local concentration lower pay.** Earnings fall 2.1% to 3.1% for the merged firm's workers and 3.3% at other employers in the hardest-hit markets.
- **Employers have power over wages.** The Treasury's review puts wages 15% to 25% below what the work adds. Factories pay 65 cents on the dollar.
- **That power costs jobs, in theory and in one fitted model.** The Treasury report states that employers with such power hire fewer workers. The one model that gives a number puts employment 0.8% to 22.5% below what it would be.
- **More concentration goes with less hiring.** 3.2% fewer hires in France and 1.6% fewer jobs in Germany for each 10% more concentration.
- **Unions and wage floors offset the power.** The wage loss is smaller where unions are strong. Germany's minimum wage raised pay and cost no jobs.
- **The central bank does act to slow the job market.** The chair said so in 2022, and the bank's own projection had unemployment rise by 0.6 points.
- **Factory markdowns rose after the early 2000s.** This is the one finding that fits "it has become worse".

## What the evidence does not support, or contradicts

- **Buyouts as a whole do not cut jobs.** The effect across all buyouts is −1.8% and not statistically different from 0. Buyouts of privately held firms add 14.9%. The earlier study found a net loss of under 1% for the firm.
- **Buyouts raise output per worker.** Revenue per worker rises 7.6 points against controls, and over 80% of that is more revenue, not fewer workers.
- **A job cut at one firm is not an idle person.** Every buyout and merger figure compares one firm with similar firms. None follows the workers to see who stays without work.
- **Most mergers do not harm wages.** Only 1.2% of mergers lower the local wage by 5% or more. Mergers that barely change concentration leave earnings unchanged. Arnold finds no sign that harmful mergers have become more common.
- **Local concentration has fallen, not risen.** It fell by nearly 600 points of HHI from the late 1970s to 2015 (Rinz), and from 0.16 to 0.11 in the model's measure. One paper, Benmelech and others, finds a rise. German concentration was stable from 2012 to 2023 by one paper seen only in a search summary.
- **Most wage-setting power does not come from consolidation.** Local concentration lowers wages by 4% to 5%. The whole wage loss is 15% to 25%. The Treasury report itself says the power comes more from frictions in the labour market, such as the cost of finding and changing jobs, than from the number of employers.
- **Unemployment was near a 50-year low while PE and mergers were large.** The yearly rate was 3.7% in 2019 and 2022 and 3.6% in 2023, the lowest since 1969. PE-backed firms had 11.7 to 13.3 million workers in those years.
- **Weaker workers go with lower unemployment, not higher.** Stansbury and Summers find a lower NAIRU where workers lost bargaining power. This runs against "unemployment is kept high to hold wages down" as a description of recent decades. It is in line with Kalecki's logic in one way: where workers are weak, less unemployment is needed to restrain wages.
- **No source shows intent to favour owners.** The central bank's stated aim is 2% inflation. The CBO rate is an estimate of where inflation starts to rise, not a target that anyone sets. Kalecki and Shapiro and Stiglitz give reasons why unemployment suits employers. They do not measure it.
- **The fall in the wage share is about pay, not hours.** It shows that a smaller part of income goes to wages. It says nothing about how many hours are worked. Autor and others trace most of it to sales moving to a few very productive firms. Berger, Herkenhoff and Mongey find that labour-market power did not cause it.
- **The Treasury's 20% is disputed.** A think tank, the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation, argues that the report picks the assumptions that give the largest figure and shows no link from concentration to the loss ([article of May 2022](https://benton.org/headlines/monopolies-are-not-taking-fifth-your-wages), opened). It gives no figure of its own.
- **Firms merge for other reasons than cutting staff.** The Belgian study finds a 10% rise in jobs after mergers classed as done for efficiency.

**Verdict on the 4 parts of the claim.**

| Part | Verdict |
|---|---|
| 1. Buyouts and mergers cut staff | True for some kinds (listed firms taken private, hospitals, mergers that raise concentration). False for buyouts as a whole |
| 2. Employers have power over wages | Supported: 15% to 25% |
| 3. That power leaves labour idle | Supported in theory and by one model, with a very wide range: 0.8% to 22.5%. A second model finds 3% of output lost and gives no figure for jobs |
| 4. Consolidation has made it worse | Not supported. Local concentration fell. Only the factory markdown series rose after the early 2000s |

## What this note must not be added to

- **Row (b) is already inside the HIGH value of the idle-capacity note.** That note's HIGH counts every unemployed person. Its LOW leaves out all unemployment up to the CBO rate. Row (b) is a part of the gap between the two. It names a cause for a part of that gap. It adds no hours.
- **Row (a) is already inside the project's band of deployable hours.** The model has no unemployed people. Its missing workers are people outside the labour force or on shorter hours. The band's participation dial goes up to 0.85 and its hours dial up to 2,080. Row (a) at its high end means 163,551 × 1.225 ÷ 341,900 = 0.586 workers per head. The band's top corner is 0.578. So the high end of row (a) is as large as the whole band and cannot sit on top of it.
- **Row (a) and row (b) overlap in part with the people who want a job and do not look.** The idle-capacity note counts 5,988 thousand of them in 2025.
- **Row (a) and row (b) may be shown side by side, not as a sum, unless the run first takes both out of the idle-capacity figures.**
- **The buyout and merger rows are not hours at all.** They are jobs moved between firms. They must not enter a run as lost hours.
- **The wage-share row is money.** It must not be turned into hours.
- **The 20.9% output loss is not 20.9% of hours.** About half of it is workers in the wrong firms. Only the employment figure of 0.8% to 22.5% is hours.

## Limits

- **Row (a) rests on one model.** The paper's employment figures are from the working-paper revision of May 2021. The published version was read only as an abstract, and its employment figure was not seen. The model removes all wage-setting power, including the part that comes from jobs being different from each other, which no policy can remove. The paper notes that the losses are smaller if higher income leads people to work less. The second model, by Lamadon and others, cannot check the figure, because it has no choice of whether to work.
- **Row (b) is an attribution.** The sources show that the central bank acts when the job market is tight. They do not show that the whole gap between the CBO rate and a floor is held there by policy. A floor of 1.3% has not been reached in any year since 1948. The lowest was 2.9%.
- **The full text of 5 papers was read from the PDF.** The fetch tool could not read the PDFs itself. It keeps a copy of each fetched file in its own results folder, outside the project. The text was read from those copies. Nothing was saved to the project.
- **Versions differ.** Divisional buyouts: −16% in the 2019 version and −0.2% in the 2024 revision. Pay: −1.7% and −3.1%. Arnold's earnings loss: 2.1% in the text and 3.1% in the abstract, which is for markets that were already concentrated.
- **Belgium, France and Germany are not the United States.** Their figures are shown for comparison only.
- **The hospital study covers 49 hospitals.** The retail report is advocacy and has no control group. The PE job counts are the industry's own.
- **Not found.**
  - Any study that measures how many people stay without work, across the whole economy, because of buyouts or mergers.
  - The employment figure in the published version of Berger, Herkenhoff and Mongey.
  - Naidu, Posner and Weyl, "Antitrust Remedies for Labor Market Power" (Harvard Law Review 2018): the paper has a figure for lost output and wage share. No opened page gave the numbers. [Paper page](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3129221) (link not verified).
  - The OECD chapter: the page refused the request.
  - The abstract of Shapiro and Stiglitz, and the original journal text of Kalecki.
  - The EY reports for the AIC, and the hospital and nursing-home papers themselves.
  - Any estimate for Germany or the European Union of hours lost to wage-setting power.
  - A statement by a central bank that it holds unemployment up to favour owners. None exists in the sources.
- **No page asked the reader to do anything.**
