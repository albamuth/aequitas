## Why this matters to Aequitas

Parecon is Aequitas's **closest living competitor**: a fully worked-out economy that uses neither markets nor a central plan, with clear rules for how people get paid and how goods get allocated. Economists have been attacking it for thirty years, which means its critics have already done — for free — a stress-test of every design choice Aequitas shares with it.

It also disagrees with Aequitas on the single most important question of all — *what work gets credited for* — which makes the comparison genuinely sharp.

## Key findings

Parecon rests on four pieces:
1. **Worker and consumer councils** — nested groups that run themselves, where your say in a decision is proportional to how much it affects you.
2. **Balanced job complexes** — the empowering tasks and the dull tasks are spread evenly across everyone, so no one gets a monopoly on the interesting, decision-making work.
3. **Pay for effort and sacrifice** — reward tracks how *long*, how *hard*, and how *unpleasant* the work was. Explicitly *not* how much you produced, *not* what you own, *not* your natural talent or your bargaining power. Your co-workers rate you, after the fact.
4. **Participatory planning** — a step-by-step procedure that replaces markets. A facilitation board publishes rough "indicative prices" (best-guess estimates of what things cost society); councils submit what they'd like to consume and produce; the board checks where demand outruns supply and nudges the prices; everyone resubmits; repeat until it settles.

**The "coordinator class" argument** — Parecon's founding historical claim. Soviet-type systems got rid of capitalists but grew a *new* ruling class anyway: the *coordinators*, who monopolised the planning and the thinking work. So abolishing income-from-property is **not enough** to abolish class.

## What we can use

- **The coordinator-class argument is the strongest outside challenge to Aequitas's positioning** — Aequitas deliberately keeps functioning institutions (government, planning offices, courts) and changes only their economic nature, which invites exactly this objection: won't the people who run those institutions become a new ruling class? It's registered as a live objection Aequitas tracks (the "coordinator class" problem, [[coordinator-class]]) and connects to the unsolved question of how contribution turns into influence — see [Foundations §10](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.38.md).
- **Unpleasant work is a gap Aequitas hasn't closed.** Parecon *pays* for onerousness. Aequitas turns exertion, skill, and danger into physical costs — a labourer eats more, training is real hours, hazard is real harm — but *tedium and indignity leave no physical trace*, so nothing in Aequitas currently makes anyone do the boring, necessary jobs. This is one of Aequitas's openly-admitted unsolved problems (see [Foundations §10](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.38.md)); the leading idea is shorter maximum hours for grim work rather than a higher rate.
- **The "indicative price" loop is a worked example** of steering production without profit. Aequitas should be able to say clearly why it doesn't need one — or borrow the idea.
- Parecon's decision rule — *your say scales with how much you're affected* — is one more candidate for how Aequitas might handle influence, and a nice one because it doesn't pile up over time.

## What it gets wrong / limitations

1. **Effort can't actually be observed.** The central criticism: rating a co-worker's "sacrifice" needs [constant surveillance and invites humiliation](https://znetwork.org/znetarticle/in-defense-of-participatory-economics-by-robin-hahnel/), and the ratings can't be checked against anything. **Aequitas is straightforwardly better here** — turning hard, skilled, and dangerous work into *material* costs (calories, training hours, health damage) needs nobody to sit in judgement of anybody's soul. State this as a genuine Aequitas advantage.
2. **Their facilitation board is a sitting duck for Hayek's objection.** [Critics argue](https://ejpe.org/journal/article/view/867) no board could realistically announce the true cost of every good, resource, job type, and piece of equipment. **Aequitas has a version of the same vulnerability:** whoever maintains its cost model (deciding what a tonne of carbon "costs," say) holds real power — that's one of Aequitas's own top open problems, the "who controls the cost model" question ([Foundations §10](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.38.md)).
3. **It's procedurally heavy.** Reaching a settled plan takes many rounds across nested councils; critics call the structure [convoluted](https://www.communistvoice.org/32cPareconStructure.html) and the meeting load unbearable. Aequitas avoids this entirely by deriving everything from a record of events rather than from deliberation — worth contrasting out loud.
4. **Balanced job complexes feel like forced labour** to [some worker critics](http://libcom.org/blog/workers-critique-parecon-11042012). Don't adopt the mechanism uncritically just because the class analysis behind it is sound.
5. **Something simpler may beat it:** some scholars argue that equal hourly pay with a cap on countable hours gets you nearly the same fairness with none of the rating apparatus — which is much closer to Aequitas's approach than to Parecon's.
