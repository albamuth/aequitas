## Rows

**Class B** is work that exists only because of money. The class is defined in [Data_sector-waste.md](Data_sector-waste.md).

**Two surveys, two totals.** The industry rows come from the payroll survey (Current Employment Statistics, a monthly count of jobs on employer payrolls). Their base is 159,293,000 nonfarm jobs, October 2026, preliminary. The occupation rows come from the occupation survey (Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics). Its newer tables would not open. The newest table that opened is May 2023, with a base of 151,853,870 jobs. Each share uses the total of its own survey.

**The job counts are published. The low and high shares are not.** No source gives the money-only share of an industry. Each low and high share below is this note's judgement, with the reason given in the section after the table. Two rules set them:

- **Low** counts a job only where the task is itself an operation on money: lending, taking payment, billing, collecting, tax work, setting premiums, investing.
- **High** adds work that exists because of profit, rent, wages or the contest for customers: selling, advertising, leasing, head offices.

An indented row is part of the row above it. A share shown in brackets is worked out from the indented rows and is not an input.

### Step 3: the two steps together

| | Every listed job counted in full | Low | High |
|---|--:|--:|--:|
| Industries (step 1) | 9.19% | 3.94% | 7.21% |
| Occupations outside them (step 2) | 9.32% | 1.31% | 5.22% |
| **Share of all US jobs, before anything is added back** | **18.5%** | **5.3%** | **12.4%** |

The middle and right columns are this note's answer. The left column is a ceiling: it counts every job in every listed row as money-only, which nobody claims.

**Worked for one person.** A typical US life takes 1,380 hours of other people's work a year. 5.3% of 1,380 is 73 hours. 12.4% of 1,380 is 171 hours. This step assumes that money-only work is the same share of those 1,380 hours as it is of all jobs. Nobody has checked that.

**The same in hours.** The payroll survey gives the average week for all employees, September 2026: banks and lenders 38.4 hours, securities 37.9, insurance 38.3, real estate 35.9, rental and leasing 34.7, legal 36.2, accounting 35.9, advertising 37.3, management of companies 38.4. All private industries average 34.4 hours (October 2026). These industries work longer weeks than average, so their share of hours is higher than their share of jobs. Weighting step 1 by hours gives **4.33% low and 7.87% high**, against 3.94% and 7.21% by jobs. The weighting uses 34.4 hours for every job in the total, because the survey gives no hours for government. The occupation survey gives no hours at all. Retail trade averages 30.2 hours, so cashiers probably count for less in hours than in jobs. **In hours the range is about 5% to 6% low and 12% to 13% high.**

**Against the thresholds in the sim design.** The design in [Problem_artificial-scarcity.md](Problem_artificial-scarcity.md) declares 15% of hours as the share that closes the US gap. Class B by itself does not reach 15% in the high case.

### Other rows that count the same people twice

- **Billing in health care.** [Data_sector-waste.md](Data_sector-waste.md) asks for a separate row for billing and insurance work in health care. The billing clerks, bookkeeping clerks and health insurers above include those people. Of the insurance carriers, 850,900 jobs are life, health and medical carriers. A health-care row must subtract them or drop them here.
- **The comparison with Germany.** The sim design warns that the US-to-Germany gap in hours already counts some waste. Finance and insurance is 4.17% of US jobs and 2.36% of German employment. Part of that gap is this class.
- **Retail salespersons** were not cut down for the step 1 industries. Few work there, and the number was not looked up.
- **Occupations where one industry was not looked up.** Financial managers in legal services and advertising; sales and marketing managers in legal and accounting services; payroll clerks in real estate. Bill collectors in real estate are not released by the survey. Each is small.
- **Self-employed people are in neither survey.** This is an undercount and not a double count. Many real estate agents, insurance agents, tax preparers, lawyers and accountants work for themselves.

## What has to be added back

A system that records hours and costs still keeps records, audits them, estimates what was not measured, and settles disputes (Foundations section 4.7). The low and high shares above already leave part of each record-keeping row in place. What follows covers the functions that were removed in full, mainly the banks' work of keeping accounts and passing payments.

## Published cross-checks, both sides

None of these measures work that exists only because of money. Each measures something near it.

### Wallis and North: the transaction sector

- **What it is.** John Wallis and Douglass North measured the resources the US economy used to make exchanges, from 1870 to 1970. The chapter is [Measuring the Transaction Sector in the American Economy, 1870-1970](https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/long-term-factors-american-economic-growth/measuring-transaction-sector-american-economy-1870-1970), in a 1986 volume edited by Engerman and Gallman. The page was opened. It gives the citation and no numbers.
- **The numbers, and how far each was checked.**
  - [Hodgson 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/24A4241CC8A307EBDF2782F410163920/core-reader), opened, reports their result as growth from 25% to 40% of economic activity over the 100 years.
  - A search summary of their own 1988 note, [Should Transaction Costs be Subtracted from Gross National Product?](https://www.econ.umd.edu/sites/www.econ.umd.edu/files/pubs/Wallis%26North_ShouldTC_JEH_1988.pdf), gives 25% of national output in 1870 and 45% in 1970. For 1870 it gives the parts: trade 16.14%, finance, insurance and real estate 4.19%, government 1.70%, transaction work inside other industries 2.16%, total 24.19%. The file is a scan and could not be read here.
  - A higher pair for the 1986 chapter, about 26% and about 55%, is often repeated. **It was not confirmed in anything opened. Do not quote it until table 3.13 of [the chapter](https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c9679/c9679.pdf) has been read by a person.**
- **What it does not show.** It is a share of output, not of hours. It ends in 1970. It counts all of wholesale and retail trade, which includes moving and storing goods. It counts managers, supervisors, clerks, police and guards in every industry, and Hodgson notes that it counts part of defence. Most of that work coordinates and informs. An economy with no money still does it. **The transaction sector is far wider than class B and is a ceiling on it at most.** For 1870, only the 4.19% for finance, insurance and real estate is close to class B.

### Graeber, and the polls behind his figure

- **The claim.** David Graeber's book *Bullshit Jobs* (2018; no link verified) holds that a large share of jobs are pointless in the view of the people who do them. The [Cambridge press release][cam], opened, reports his range as 20% to 50% of the workforce, and possibly 60%.
- **The United Kingdom poll.** [YouGov, August 2015][yg-uk], opened: 37% of working British adults said their job does not make "a meaningful contribution to the world". 50% said it does and 13% were unsure. The article gives no sample size. A search summary gives 849 workers and a later repeat at 33%.
- **The United States poll.** [YouGov, August 2015][yg-us], opened: 24% said their job is not meaningful and 63% said it is.
- **What the polls do not show.** They ask for a feeling about one's own job. They are online panels. A banker can feel useful and a nurse can feel useless. Nothing in them is about money.

### Soffia, Wood and Burchell: the critical test

- **The paper.** Magdalena Soffia, Alex Wood and Brendan Burchell, [Alienation Is Not 'Bullshit': An Empirical Critique of Graeber's Theory of BS Jobs](https://doi.org/10.1177/09500170211015067), *Work, Employment and Society* 36(5), 2022, pages 816 to 840 (link not verified: the publisher refused the request). The [Birmingham repository page](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/alienation-is-not-bullshit-an-empirical-critique-of-graebers-theo/) was opened and gives the abstract without numbers. The numbers below are from the [Cambridge press release][cam], opened.
- **The finding.** In the European Working Conditions Survey, the share of European Union workers who felt their work was not useful was 7.8% in 2005 and 4.8% in 2015. The United Kingdom and Ireland were at 5.6%. The samples were over 21,000 people in 2005 and almost 30,000 in 2015.
- **By occupation.** Refuse collectors 9.7%, cleaners and helpers 8.1%, sales workers 7.7%. Graeber's theory predicts that finance and administration staff should score highest. The release names manual and sales jobs instead.
- **What it does not show.** The same limit as the polls: it is a feeling. It tests Graeber's theory and it does not measure money-only work.

### A third survey: Dur and van Lent

- Robert Dur and Max van Lent, [Socially Useless Jobs](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/11927/socially-useless-jobs), IZA Discussion Paper 11927, 2018, later in *Industrial Relations* 58(1), 2019. The page was opened. About 8% of workers see their job as socially useless and another 17% are doubtful. The data cover 100,000 workers in 47 countries at four dates.

### How the cross-checks sit beside this note

| Measure | Share | What it is a share of |
|---|--:|---|
| Soffia, Wood and Burchell, European Union, 2015 | 4.8% | Workers who feel their work is not useful |
| **This note, low** | **5.3%** | US jobs |
| Dur and van Lent | 8% | Workers who see their job as socially useless |
| **This note, high** | **12.4%** | US jobs |
| YouGov, United States, 2015 | 24% | Workers who say their job is not meaningful |
| YouGov, United Kingdom, 2015 | 37% | Workers who say their job is not meaningful |

**The numbers being close proves nothing.** The surveys and this note measure different things.

## Limits

- **The low and high shares are judgement, not data.** The sim design in [Problem_artificial-scarcity.md](Problem_artificial-scarcity.md) warns that whoever picks the shares picks the result. The job counts meet the rule for a row. The shares do not. They were written before any run. A run should also report the ceiling of 18.5%, so a reader sees how far the result depends on the shares.
- **Four rows make up a third of the high case, and none has a published split.** Cashiers add 1.62 points, management of companies 0.83, sales representatives and managers 1.01, retail salespersons 0.61. Together that is 4.07 of the 12.4 points.
- **Jobs are not hours.** A job is one person on one payroll, full time or part time. Hours were available for industries and not for occupations.
- **Two surveys and two dates.** Industry rows are October and September 2026. Occupation rows are May 2023, because the May 2024 and May 2025 occupation tables would not open. A search summary gives 3,148,030 cashiers for May 2024, which is 150,630 fewer than the May 2023 figure used here.
- **Self-employed people are missing** from both US surveys. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projections table, which counts them, gives 170,280,800 jobs in 2025 against 159,293,000 on payrolls. The German figures do count the self-employed.
- **Work not looked up:** tax collectors in federal and state government beyond the 50,250 tax examiners; buyers and purchasing agents; pay and benefits specialists; telephone call centres (307,000 jobs, September 2026); security guards who protect cash and goods (1,202,940 jobs, May 2023); the unpaid hours households spend on tax forms and bills.
- **Germany is a comparison and not a second estimate.** No low or high share was set for it. The German classes do not match the US ones:

| Group | US share of jobs | German share of employed persons | Why they do not match |
|---|--:|--:|---|
| Finance and insurance | 4.17% | 2.36% | German public health funds are counted under social security, in public administration. US private health insurers are counted here |
| Real estate | 1.16% (real estate only; 1.53% with rental of goods) | 1.14% | The German class leaves out rental of cars and equipment |
| Legal, accounting and head offices | 3.14% | 3.43% | The German class adds management consultancy. The US figure is legal 0.79% + accounting 0.71% + management of companies 1.65% |
| Advertising | 0.30% | 0.45% | The German class adds market research |

- **Germany in hours, 2024, provisional:** finance and insurance 1,592,928 thousand hours of 61,817,489 thousand, which is 2.58%. Real estate 680,536 thousand, which is 1.10%. Eurostat gives no hours for the finer classes.
- **Wants are held fixed.** If lending and advertising stop, people may want different things. This note does not model that.
- **The move from all jobs to one person's 1,380 hours is an assumption.** Some US work goes to exports, to buildings and machines, and to government. The 1,380 hours count the work behind what one household consumes.
