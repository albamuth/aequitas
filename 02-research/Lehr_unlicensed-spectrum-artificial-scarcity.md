## Why this matters to Aequitas

Radio spectrum is the range of radio frequencies that transmitters use. Nobody made it, and a licence is an exclusive right to transmit in one band. That makes it a close cousin of land, which Aequitas says cannot be owned ([Foundations §3.7](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations.md#37-land-is-not-owned-a-building-carries-a-remediation-debt)). Aequitas has no rule for spectrum. This paper is a mainstream economist's statement that spectrum scarcity is mostly made by rules, and it also says where real scarcity remains. It tests the author's claim of 2026-11-19 that scarcity today is largely artificial.

## Key findings

**The paper's thesis is narrower than "scarcity is artificial."** Lehr accepts that most valuable spectrum will go to tradable exclusive licences. He argues that more of the valuable low-frequency spectrum should also be left open to all (pp. 2, 8). The claim about artificial scarcity is his starting premise. He does not set out to prove it.

- **The premise (p. 1).** Regulators give narrow bands to named uses and users. The licences fix the technology and the business, and they block moving a band to a better use. Lehr says the resulting shortage comes from the rules and not from technical limits. In his words: "This scarcity is largely artificial" (p. 1).
- **Two effects on opportunity cost (p. 1).** Opportunity cost is what a band could have produced in its best other use. New users face a cost that is too high: no access at all, or a large auction fee. Holders face a cost that is too low, so they have little reason to use their band more sparingly. Lehr names government users and over-the-air broadcasters as the holders.
- **A second source of artificial scarcity: the holder (p. 5).** A holder of an exclusive licence can keep others out even when the band has room. Lehr says a holder will do this whenever it suits the holder's private interest. Two cases, both reported second-hand:
  - US broadcasters opposed new broadcasting licences. This slowed UHF television and FM radio. Lehr's source is Thomas Hazlett's 2001 essay in the Harvard Journal of Law and Technology (no link; not read).
  - Mobile carriers in some markets opposed more 3G licences because they feared competition. No source is given.
- **Holders also resist reform (pp. 8, 10).** Licence holders oppose new sharing rights in their bands. They also oppose new cheap spectrum that rivals could use. Lehr describes all parties as bargaining over the gains that reform would release.
- **Lock-in after the fact (p. 9).** If a new device turns out to work only in one band, the licence holder of that band can charge its users. Lehr asks the reader to consider whether Wi-Fi would have grown if buyers had faced unknown future fees from a spectrum holder.
- **The one success case: Wi-Fi (p. 11).** Wi-Fi runs in the 2.4 GHz band. That band was thought worthless because microwave ovens and other devices already filled it with noise. Lehr reports that Wi-Fi equipment and networks grew exponentially. He gives no figures.
- **Where the value sits (pp. 2, 10).** The valuable spectrum is below 3 GHz, and most of all below 1 GHz. Frequencies differ physically in antenna size, in how far a signal travels, and in whether it needs a clear line of sight.
- **How open bands handle interference (pp. 3–4, 7, 9–10).** Interference is what happens when two signals on one frequency in one place garble each other. The paper lists four ways open bands manage it:
  - **Power limits.** A device that transmits weakly reaches only a short distance, so it disturbs few others. Ultra-wideband radio spreads a weak signal over a very wide band (p. 7).
  - **Listen before talking.** A device checks that the band is quiet and only then transmits (p. 7).
  - **Shared protocols.** Users or industry standards bodies can choose the rules of access, so the government does not have to pick the technology (pp. 3–4).
  - **Tolerant equipment.** Devices that cope with noise do well in open bands, and uses that need quiet move elsewhere (pp. 9–10).
- **Technology has made spectrum less scarce (pp. 8–9).** Better signal processing, antennas and modulation carry more information in the same band. Radios that change frequency by software are less tied to one band.
- **No country runs on open access alone (p. 6, footnote 14).** Lehr cites Guatemala as the rare case of a full move to tradable licences. He says no country has moved fully to the open model.

## What we can use

- **A citable statement of the premise, from inside the mainstream.** Lehr is an MIT economist who favours markets. He still says the shortage is mostly made by rules (p. 1) and that holders can make more of it (p. 5). This fits the first row of the table in [Foundations §4.6](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations.md), which says much scarcity is produced by holding supply back. Cite it as an expert's opinion. It is not a measurement.
- **The mechanism of exclusion, stated plainly.** An exclusive right lets its holder shut out users at no cost to the holder, even when there is room. This is the same mechanism as the patent in [Foundations §4.5](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations.md) and the land title in §3.7. The Wi-Fi fee example (p. 9) shows it with a real product.
- **A starting point for a spectrum rule.** These are this note's inferences. Lehr does not make them.
  - **The spectrum itself has no cost.** Nobody worked to make it, so the cost rule of [Foundations §0](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations.md) records nothing for it. The hours and the material are in the radios, the towers and the electricity. An auction fee has no matching work, so Aequitas has nowhere to record it.
  - **The land analogy holds for exclusion and fails for remediation.** A transmitter occupies a band in one place only while it is on. When it stops, the band is as it was. A building leaves ground to restore; a transmitter leaves nothing. A spectrum rule would carry no remediation debt of the §3.7 kind.
  - **Real congestion already has a matching rule.** Foundations §4.6 says a physically scarce thing goes by a queue or a lottery at the point of handover. Listen-before-talking is a queue run by the devices themselves. The paper also notes that society allocates some scarce things without a price, and gives ambulances as its example (p. 4).
  - **Interference leaves a physical trace.** Signal power at a place and time can be measured. Under the [physical-trace test](../01-wiki/physical-trace-test.md), sharing a crowded band could be settled by measurement and would not need a convention.
- **A warning about the cure.** Lehr's remedy for congestion is smarter radios. Those take work, material and energy to build. Under Aequitas that cost would appear as debit on the equipment. Open access moves part of the cost from a fee to hardware.

## What it gets wrong / limitations

- **It is an opinion essay with no data.** There are no measurements of how much spectrum sits idle. There are no auction figures and no Wi-Fi sales figures. The only numbers are frequency marks: 3 GHz, 1 GHz and 2.4 GHz. The word "largely" has no number behind it.
- **The evidence for holders blocking entry is second-hand.** The broadcaster case rests on one citation that this note did not read. The 3G case has no citation at all.
- **It is advocacy.** The paper says it makes the counter case for open spectrum (p. 2). Lehr wrote an earlier paper for the same cause: ["The Economic Case for Dedicated Unlicensed Spectrum Below 3GHz" (New America, 2004)](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/the-economic-case-for-dedicated-unlicensed-spectrum-below-3ghz/) (link from a search result, not opened).
- **It is 21 years old.** In 2005 ultra-wideband and frequency-sensing radios were unproven, and Lehr says so (p. 8). This note has not checked what happened to open spectrum after 2005.
- **The venue is unknown.** The paper carries a date and no publisher. It may never have passed peer review.
- **Its remedy is the opposite of Aequitas.** Lehr wants more market, not less. He supports tradable licences, auctions during the change-over, and resale markets (pp. 1, 4). He treats opportunity cost and a market price as the right guides. Aequitas has no rent and no profit in exchange. The paper supports the diagnosis only.
- **It does not show that open access alone can work.** Lehr calls a move to open access only neither desirable nor politically feasible (p. 2 and footnote 7). He also says Wi-Fi may not be good enough for a basic access network (p. 11).
- **It does not solve enforcement.** The paper says open bands still need rules to limit interference (pp. 3–4). It does not say who sets the power limit or who stops a device that ignores it.
- **It says nothing about food, housing or energy.** Spectrum is unlike them in three ways.
  - It is not used up. A band is whole again the moment a transmitter stops. A loaf, once eaten, is gone.
  - It takes no work to make. Food, houses and power take hours, material and energy.
  - Better equipment lets the same band carry more. No change of rule makes a field grow more wheat.

  [Foundations §3.5](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations.md) measures the limit on made goods. Deployable work hours come to 0.43 – 0.87 of what a median US lifestyle needs. Energy comes to 0.19 of need in the self-sufficient US scenario. No licensing reform changes those figures. Spectrum is the easiest case for the claim, and it cannot stand in for goods that must be made. The closest matches are land and patents, where exclusion is a legal right over something nobody made or something that copies freely.

## Against the claim that scarcity is artificial

The author's claim has three parts. The source bears on each one differently.

| Part of the claim | What this source does |
|---|---|
| Scarcity today is largely artificial | **Supports it for spectrum only**, as an expert's opinion without a measurement. **Does not address** any other good. |
| Much is held by a few | **Partly supports it.** The holders it names are government users and broadcasters (p. 1). Government is not a private holder. The paper gives no figures on who holds how much. |
| Rules written for the holders make it worse | **Partly supports it, partly contradicts it.** Holders lobby against new licences and against reform (pp. 5, 8, 10). The paper blames the old rules mainly on technology that is a century out of date (p. 1). It does not say the rules were written for the holders. |

Points in the source that limit or contradict the claim:

- **Real scarcity exists, and no rule removes it (p. 4).** Lehr says the choice of regime cannot end real scarcity. Real scarcity arises whenever more uses compete for a band than it can fit.
- **He defines real scarcity precisely (p. 5, footnote 12).** It exists when adding one more transmitter would cost society more than it gains. The cause can be technical: the added signal would wreck the others. It can be economic: sharing is possible, and the equipment changes cost more than the sharing is worth. The footnote as printed has benefit and cost the wrong way round; this note gives the evident meaning.
- **Some bands are already full (p. 11).** Lehr says mobile service bands are at capacity in many markets. That is real scarcity, under the very licences he criticises.
- **Open bands get crowded too (pp. 4, 9–10).** Congestion lowers quality for everyone in the band. Lehr says this loss acts on users much as a price would. He names the risk of a Tragedy of the Commons, in which each user's overuse spoils a shared thing for all.
- **Exclusive licences do a real job (pp. 5–6).** The exclusive right protects a user's signal from interference. A single operator can coordinate a network across a whole city. Lehr grants that some wide-area services may prefer this protection (p. 7).
- **Removing the rules may not make spectrum cheap (p. 1, footnote 2).** Freeing spectrum also raises demand. Lehr says the future cost of spectrum cannot be predicted in advance.
- **Low frequencies are physically better (pp. 2, 10).** Signals below 1 GHz travel farther and pass obstacles. There is only so much of that range. This is a scarcity of position, like a good site for a building, and no rule makes more of it.
- **Much of the gain came from invention (pp. 8–9).** Lehr credits signal processing, antennas and software radios with making spectrum less scarce. That gain took engineering work. A change of rules alone did not produce it.
- **The open model is a small share (p. 11).** Lehr says Wi-Fi carried far less activity than licensed mobile services in 2005.
