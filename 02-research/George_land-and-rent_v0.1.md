## Why this matters to Aequitas

George is the most famous argument that **land is different from everything else you can own** — that the gains of a growing society flow to whoever holds the land, as rent, and that this is the engine of poverty amid plenty. Aequitas reaches a stronger version of the same intuition: **land can't be owned at all.** George is the citable ancestor for Aequitas's treatment of land.

Bears on: [Foundations §3.7 (land is not owned; a building carries a remediation debt)](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.35.md#37-land-is-not-owned-a-building-carries-a-remediation-debt).

## Key findings

- **Rent captures the gains of progress.** As technology and population advance, George argued, land *values* rise faster than wages — so the extra wealth a society produces is siphoned off by landowners as rent, rather than reaching workers. That, he said, is why poverty persists even as a country grows richer.
- **Land speculation drives the boom-bust cycle** — holding land idle in expectation of higher rents starves productive use and helps trigger recessions.
- **The remedy: a single tax on land value.** Tax the *unimproved value of land* heavily enough to replace all other taxes ("Georgism"). Because you can't hide or move land, the tax is hard to dodge, doesn't discourage building or working (only hoarding), and returns the community-created value of land to the community.
- Underlying it all: **land is a common resource** — nobody made it, so no one has a natural claim to pocket the rent from it.

## What we can use

- **George is the pedigree for "you don't own the land."** Aequitas takes his intuition to its conclusion: land isn't taxed, it's *un-ownable*. A building doesn't sit on property you hold a deed to; it occupies a patch of Earth, and occupying it is a debt — the cost of one day restoring that patch to its natural state. See [Foundations §3.7](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.35.md#37-land-is-not-owned-a-building-carries-a-remediation-debt).
- **No rent, by construction.** Where George needed a *tax* to claw back land rent, Aequitas removes the rent at the root: there is no profit in exchange and holding property is a burden, not an income (see [Foundations §7.1](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.35.md)). You cannot earn by holding land, so there is nothing to tax back.
- **"Community-created value returns to the community"** maps onto Aequitas's rule that cleaning up and restoring the commons lightens *everyone's* record — value tied to shared land isn't privately pocketed.

## What it gets wrong / limitations — from Aequitas's angle

- **His remedy is still a tax, inside a money economy.** George keeps private landholding and money rent, then taxes the rent away. Aequitas needs neither the money rent nor the tax — it dissolves the category of land-ownership instead. Take the diagnosis; the single-tax machinery is unnecessary here.
- **George values land in money.** Aequitas doesn't price land at all — it prices the *physical* cost of restoring the ground (contamination cleared, foundations pulled, the hole filled). That sidesteps the hard question of "what is this land worth?" which George still had to answer.
- **The "natural state" baseline is genuinely hard.** Deciding what a plot "was like naturally" — after centuries of a city — is a real judgement call, handled the same careful way as "how much pollution can the world absorb." An open edge, flagged honestly. See [Foundations §10](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.35.md).
