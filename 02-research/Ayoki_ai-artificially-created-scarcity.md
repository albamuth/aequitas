## Why this matters to Aequitas
The title suggests support for the claim that scarcity is man-made. The paper does not argue that. "Artificially" is a play on "artificial intelligence": the paper says cheap machine thinking moves scarcity to carbon, human time and calendar time. It is useful mainly as a contrast for the Aequitas baseline, and as a warning about what this title can and cannot be cited for.

## Key findings
- **The thesis (abstract; section 1, p. 1).** Artificial intelligence (AI) pushes the cost of one more unit of thinking, coordinating and designing toward zero. Scarcity stays and changes place. The paper names three scarce things: the room left in the atmosphere for carbon, human time, and calendar time.
- **The main conclusion is about the discount rate, not about scarcity.** The social discount rate is the yearly percentage by which a public body counts a future benefit as worth less than the same benefit today. The paper says it must fall to zero (abstract; section 4.4, p. 14; section 5, p. 15). Its central estimate for 2040 is 0.07% a year, with a 95% range of −0.2% to 0.4%.
- **Production (section 3.1, p. 6).** Output comes from two inputs that can replace each other. One is low-carbon equipment multiplied by an AI factor that grows without limit. The other is a bundle of energy and labour. The paper assumes the two replace each other easily (its parameter σ is above 1). Under that assumption machines can replace the whole energy-and-labour bundle.
- **Carbon (section 3.2, p. 7).** Carbon in the air rises with fossil energy use and falls by natural uptake and by AI-assisted removal. The cost of removing one more tonne is assumed to fall every year at a steady rate. The section heading says carbon goes from scarce to plentiful under management. The carbon price ends up equal to the cost of removing a tonne.
- **Labour (section 3.3, p. 8).** The share of income paid as wages falls by a simple equation driven by the AI factor. Displaced workers wait longer and longer for new jobs. The paper says a permanent group of workers forms whose work adds nothing to output. Output keeps growing while hours of work collapse. Work becomes voluntary.
- **Welfare (section 3.4, pp. 8-9).** One household stands for everybody. Its welfare rises with consumption and falls with work and with carbon in the air. The paper sets the pure rate of time preference (how much less the planner cares about next year than this year) to 0.000001 a year.
- **Result 1, energy cost (section 4.1, pp. 11-12).** The paper reports a cost-fall rate of 0.33 a year (standard error 0.04) from a panel of 38 countries, 5 technologies and weekly data for 2020 to 2024, with 39,420 rows. It projects solar-plus-storage electricity at 19.2 to 20.1 dollars per megawatt-hour in 2030 and 13.2 to 14.5 in 2040.
- **Result 2, wages (section 4.2, pp. 12-13).** The wage share of income falls to 0.18 by 2050 (middle half of runs: 0.16 to 0.21). With slow AI it is 0.26.
- **Result 3, the transfer (section 4.2.2, p. 13; Table 4-3).** Lower wages shrink the tax base. The paper puts the gap in public budgets at 12.0% of gross domestic product in 2050. It compares three policies. An AI dividend is an equal payment to everybody, paid from a 30% tax on AI cash flow: the gap becomes −0.2%. A 20% robot tax leaves a gap of 4.8% and cuts output by 3%. Retirement at 70 leaves 7.5%. With no reform the poorest tenth loses 18.2% of welfare and the richest tenth loses 2.1%. With the dividend the poorest tenth gains 4.7% and the richest gains 1.3%.
- **Result 4, long projects (section 4.3, pp. 13-14).** The paper lists 47 projects that take more than 100 years to pay back, such as carbon removal and asteroid defence. It says that once AI cuts building time below 20 years, starting at once beats waiting for all 47.
- **The paper states the condition that would defeat it (section 4.4, p. 14; Table 4-1).** If the cost-fall rate is 0.15 a year and not 0.33, the discount rate settles at 1.1% and the zero result is gone.
- **Policy list (section 5, pp. 15-18).** A zero discount rate for long public projects. A 2% charge on the revenue of very large AI training runs, paid into a national fund that pays a quarterly dividend to every legal resident. A carbon price that peaks at 150 US dollars a tonne in 2035 and falls to 50 by 2050. A new central bank that issues 100-year bonds.
- **AI's own footprint (section 1, p. 1, no source given).** Data centres emit 0.4 to 1.6 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent a year by 2035. AI could avoid 3.2 to 5.4 billion tonnes a year by 2035.

## What we can use
- **A contrast for the baseline, in two rows.** The paper answers the fall in wages with a money transfer. Aequitas answers with hours counted for every living person (Foundations section 5.5.3, the floor `F`, the hours credited each day for being alive: [Aequitas_Foundations.md](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations.md)).

  | | AI dividend (this paper) | Aequitas baseline |
  |---|---|---|
  | What a person gets | Money, or tradeable assets in a digital wallet (carbon credits, land tokens, shares) | Hours of credit recorded on their own account. Nothing moves between accounts |
  | Where it comes from | A tax on AI firms: 30% of cash flow in section 4.2.2, 2% of revenue in section 5 | Nowhere. It is a record, set by the network |
  | What it depends on | AI firms keeping large profits to tax | The network's setting, between the essentials basket and the point where the books stop rationing |
  | Who is covered | Every legal resident of one country | Every verified person in a network |
  | Can it pile up or be traded | Yes | No. Credit cannot be lent, moved or sold |

  **Worked example.** In the paper, the dividend exists only while there is AI profit to tax. If AI profit halves, a 30% tax raises half as much, and the dividend halves. In Aequitas a floor of 10 hours a day gives 3,650 hours a year whatever any firm earns.
- **The dividend needs the thing Aequitas removes.** It is funded from what the paper calls compute rents, the income from owning scarce computing capacity. Aequitas allows no seller to add profit, rent or interest. So the paper's remedy cannot be copied into Aequitas. This is worth one line in the Objections register under "why not a universal basic income".
- **A second voice for one accounting idea, and no more than that.** In section 3.2 the carbon price falls to the cost of removing a tonne. That matches the Aequitas rule that pollution is counted by the work of cleaning it up ([no-externalities](../01-wiki/no-externalities.md)). Cite it as agreement on the idea. Do not cite its numbers.
- **A sentence on concentrated ownership.** The paper says its production weight α is an index of property rights over AI output, and that conflict over shares follows if ownership of AI stays concentrated (section 3.1, p. 6; section 3.3, p. 8). This fits the Foundations section 4.6 line that much scarcity is produced. It is stated, and the paper does not test it.
- **A test the paper offers against itself.** It says the yield on 50-year inflation-indexed government bonds should follow its predicted rate, near 0.07% by 2040. That is a check a reader can run.
- **Do not cite this paper as evidence for any number.** See the next section.

## What it gets wrong / limitations
- **Not peer-reviewed, and no data or code is published.** None of the four results can be re-run.
- **The figures are missing.** Figures 4-1 to 4-5 appear only as a list of captions on page 21. Table 4-3 has no row for retirement at 70, which the text quotes.
- **Several references could not be found, and some cannot exist as cited.**
  - The paper cites Cowen and Parfit, 2024, in the journal *Ethics*. Derek Parfit died in 2017. A search found only their 1992 chapter "Against the social discount rate" ([listing](https://stafforini.com/works/cowen-1992-social-discount-rate/), link not verified).
  - It cites a 2023 NeurIPS keynote by Ilya Sutskever on time as the last scarcity. A search found no such talk. His NeurIPS talk that was reported in the press was in December 2024 and was about limits on training data.
  - It cites Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, 2023, for a 23% fall in earnings across 5.2 million Upwork contracts. The 2023 paper by those three authors that a search finds is [Generative AI at Work](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161). It studies about 5,179 customer-support agents and reports a 14% rise in issues resolved per hour. It is not about Upwork.
  - Kozyrkov (2024) and Nordhaus (2017) are cited in the text and are absent from the reference list.
  - The other references were not checked. Treat every cited figure as unverified.
- **The copyright line reads 2009** on a paper dated 2025.
- **The paper contradicts itself on its own numbers.**
  - The wage share falls under 20% by 2050 in section 4.2. Section 5 says it falls under 45% by 2040. The paper does not connect the two figures.
  - The dividend is funded by a 30% tax on AI cash flow in section 4.2.2 and by a 2% charge on training-run revenue in section 5.
  - Section 3.4 sets the pure rate of time preference to almost zero as an input. Section 4 then says the zero discount rate holds even when that rate is positive.
- **The headline result is built in.** Section 4.4 adds a new term to the standard formula for the discount rate. The term is the value of finishing long projects sooner. The paper then sets that term, from its own option model, to equal the rest of the formula by 2040. The answer of about zero follows from that choice. This is the kind of check Foundations section 3.5 excludes: the person running it supplies the inputs that decide the verdict.
- **The panel size does not add up (my arithmetic).** 38 countries × 5 technologies = 190 cells. 2020 to 2024 is about 260 weeks. 190 × 260 = 49,400 rows. The paper reports 39,420 and does not explain the difference.
- **One claim in section 5 fails a simple check (my arithmetic).** The paper says a zero rate makes climate damage in the year 2100 worth 4 times as much today as under a 3% rate. At 3% a year over the 75 years from 2025 to 2100, the factor is 1.03^75, about 9.
- **One household stands for everybody.** The welfare model in section 3.4 has a single representative household. The split between owners and workers appears only as a one-line extension (section 3.6, p. 10) and as Table 4-3, whose model is described in three lines.
- **Money and prices are kept.** The paper keeps profit, rent, bonds, taxes and a central bank. It measures cost in dollars. It has no counterpart to counting cost in hours of work.
- **Physical quantities are thin.** Carbon is the only physical stock in the model. Land, water and the amount of energy that can be built are not modelled. Critical minerals appear as one row in a table.

## Against the claim that scarcity is artificial
The claim under test (2026-11-19): scarcity today is largely artificial; productive capacity exceeds consumption; much is hoarded by a few; much is wasted on war, luxury, planned obsolescence, surplus dumping and needless cheap goods.

**What the source supports, weakly**
- Concentrated ownership can make most people poorer while output grows. In the no-reform case the poorest tenth loses 18.2% of welfare. This is a forecast for 2050 from a model nobody can re-run. It is not a measurement of today.
- One sentence says that open AI model weights would prevent an artificial scarcity (section 2, p. 5). The paper credits the point to a 2020 book by Abbott (reference not checked) and does not develop it.

**What the source does not address**
- Whether today's productive capacity exceeds today's consumption. The paper is about 2030 to 2050.
- Hoarding of goods, war, luxury, planned obsolescence, surplus dumping and cheap goods. None of these words or topics appears.
- Supply held back to keep a price up, which is the meaning of "produced scarcity" in Foundations section 4.6.

**What in the source limits or contradicts the claim**
- **The title does not mean what the claim means.** The scarcity in the paper is caused by a technology changing what is short. Nobody withholds anything. Citing this paper for "scarcity is man-made" would misreport it.
- **The paper says scarcity does not end.** Its first page says abundance of thinking changes scarcity and does not remove it. Its last page says scarcity will be measured in time.
- **Carbon space is treated as a physical limit with fixed dates.** The paper speaks of hard physical deadlines for emissions (section 1, p. 2). No change of ownership moves that limit.
- **Time is treated as a limit no one can produce.** This agrees with Foundations section 0 and section 5.5.5, where nobody has more than 24 hours a day. It is a real limit, not an artificial one.
- **The abundance is conditional.** At a cost-fall rate of 0.15 a year the paper's own result fails (Table 4-1). The paper is therefore not evidence that abundance already exists.
- **AI adds to the carbon problem as well as easing it.** Data centres emit 0.4 to 1.6 billion tonnes a year by 2035 on the paper's own figures.

**Where the source disagrees with Aequitas itself**
- **Human working hours.** Foundations section 3.5 measures that the world's working hours cover about 0.63 of what a typical United States life for everybody would need. The paper says the opposite for the future: hours of work collapse and output still grows. That rests on one assumption, that machines replace labour and energy easily (σ above 1). The paper does not test that assumption. The summary we were given said labour hours stay a binding limit. In the model they do not. "Human time" in the paper means the period in which human decisions still matter, and the value of leisure.
- **Energy and minerals.** Foundations section 3.5 finds energy and critical minerals the tightest physical limit, with energy at 0.19 of what is needed at the current build. The paper's mineral test raises the 2030 electricity cost from about 20 to 22 dollars per megawatt-hour and stops there. A cost per unit says nothing about how many units can be built. The two results are not measuring the same thing, and the paper's is not a reply to ours.
- **If the paper were right about labour, the Aequitas unit would need a second look.** A thing made almost wholly by machines would cost very few hours. The floor would then buy far more, and the tolerance (the share of a person's counted hours that their consumption may reach) would be the only brake. This is a question for a later stress test. The paper gives no evidence strong enough to force it now.
