## Why this matters to Aequitas

Polanyi supplies the best-established academic argument that **land, labour, and money are not really commodities** — and Aequitas independently reaches the same three conclusions, one axiom at a time. This is a strong, citable ally: a respected economic historian saying, eighty years ago, that treating these three things as ordinary goods for sale "subordinates the substance of society itself to the laws of the market."

Bears on: land can't be owned ([Foundations §3.7](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.32.md#37-land-is-not-owned-a-building-carries-a-remediation-debt)); labour is time spent, never rate-scaled or priced as a commodity ([Foundations §A2](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.32.md#a2-time-as-measure)); and money-wealth is not material and carries no cost ([Foundations §A1](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.32.md#a1-materialism-of-cost)).

## Key findings

- **The economy is "embedded" in society.** For most of history, Polanyi argues, economic life was bound up in social relationships — reciprocity, custom, redistribution — not a separate self-running market sphere. The self-regulating market is a recent and deliberate invention, not humanity's natural state. (This directly counters the "people always trucked and bartered" origin story economists inherit from Adam Smith.)
- **The three "fictitious commodities."** Land, labour, and money get *treated* as things produced for sale, but none of them actually is: land is nature, labour is human life, money is a token of trust. Running them through the market as if they were ordinary goods does violence to the things themselves.
- **The "double movement."** When markets try to tear free of society, society pushes back with protective measures (labour law, land regulation, welfare). History swings between marketisation and this protective reaction — commentators still speak of "Polanyi moments" in crises like 2008.

## What we can use

- **Polanyi is external corroboration for three separate Aequitas rules at once.** Aequitas reaches "land, labour, money aren't commodities" not as one grand claim but as three consequences of its axioms:
  - **Land** — you can't own it; a building instead carries a debt to restore the patch of Earth it occupies. See [Foundations §3.7](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.32.md#37-land-is-not-owned-a-building-carries-a-remediation-debt).
  - **Labour** — credit counts *time spent*, the same for everyone's hour; it is never rate-scaled or sold. See [Foundations §A2](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.32.md#a2-time-as-measure).
  - **Money** — Aequitas counts only *material* things; a share, a bond, a token is a paper claim, not matter or energy, so it carries no cost at all. See [Foundations §A1](../00-strategy/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.32.md#a1-materialism-of-cost).
  That a mainstream historian names exactly these three, on independent grounds, is a gift to the academic write-up.
- **"Embeddedness" reframes the whole project positively.** Aequitas isn't tearing the economy out of society — it's the opposite of the "great transformation," putting economic accounting back inside physical and social reality rather than letting an abstract market float above it.
- **The barter-myth point** is shared with Graeber (see [Graeber: Debt](../00-strategy/GLOSSARY.md#src-graeber-debt)) and undercuts the standard origin story that props up money's necessity.

## What it gets wrong / limitations — from Aequitas's angle

- **Diagnosis, not mechanism.** Polanyi shows the disease (society subordinated to the market) but proposes no worked accounting system. Aequitas has to supply the machinery he lacks.
- **His "protection" is state regulation.** Polanyi's double movement leans on the state pushing back against the market. Aequitas doesn't rely on a regulator noticing — the protection is built into what the accounting *is* (a cost can't be dumped because it's a physical flow the ledger records). Take the diagnosis; leave the statism.
- Polanyi is sometimes read as romanticising pre-market societies. Aequitas needn't — its claim is forward-looking, not nostalgic.
