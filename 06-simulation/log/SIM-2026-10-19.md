## Verdict
**Between the best- and worst-instrumented producer of one good, a typical early-leaving good reads about 2× apart (median 1.96 to 2.08 at every chain length from 2 to 30 steps). So the spread does not widen with chain length for the typical good. The average spread does widen, from 1.88 at n=2 to 4.70 at n=30 (slope 1.02 per ln n, 95% interval 0.74 to 1.34), because a longer chain lets a good leave very early.** In hours: the best producer reads about 0.46 h/kg and the worst about 1.0 h/kg, a gap near 0.4 h/kg. At the default settings 32% of producers read more than 1.5× their true figure. **This size depends on an affordability cap I chose (producers spend 2% of their labour on instruments). It does not yet show that §4.8's offset is, or is not, enough.** Nothing in the documents moves.

## What this does not show
1. **The affordability rule is mine.** A producer taxed 0.5 h/kg on 10⁴ kg would rationally pay 40 h for a cut. If producers bought whenever the saving beat the price, the share paying more than 1.5× would fall toward zero. The result measures a budget cap.
2. **The tax lands on the buyer, not the producer.** How a heavy-reading good loses buyers, and §4.8's offset (helping someone join is credited work), are not modelled.
3. **Exit points and step costs are invented.** The mean's growth with ln(n) is the harmonic-number effect of uniform exits. It disappears if goods leave at a fixed share of chain cost. No real producer data was used.
4. **A false passage claim is taken as impossible.** That is the open verification question already in §3.4a.
5. **No shared instruments or measurement services.** A trade body or contract service would flatten the spread.
