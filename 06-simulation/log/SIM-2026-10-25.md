## Verdict
**Neither dominates; it depends on where goods leave the chain and on one number I had to calibrate.** With the efficiency spread set to the 1.82× bar (1,380 h ÷ 759 h per year), the typical producer's resolution spread is **smaller** than the efficiency spread: p90/p10 of **1.73** against **2.29** (n = 12). The mean and the variance go the other way: resolution is **79%** of the variance, because goods that leave a long chain very early carry 10× to 20× penalties (mean p90/p10 **4.84**). If goods leave in the last third of the chain, resolution is noise: **1.16** against **2.30**. The answer flips for efficiency spreads between SIG_A 0.2 and 0.7, so the weakest input is the efficiency spread, which no data here pins for a single good. **It moves nothing in the documents yet.** It does show SB-06 §7's "flat chain returns near 1.0" is wrong when goods leave anywhere in the chain (1.71).

## What this does not show
1. The efficiency spread is calibrated to a whole-lifestyle national ratio, not observed for any single good. The verdict flips inside the plausible range of that number.
2. Exit positions and step costs are invented. Exit position moves the answer from "noise" to "comparable".
3. The affordability cap (2% of labour, 40 h per cut) is mine and decides how many producers pay.
4. Efficiency and scale are independent here. In the world they likely move together.
5. Using 1.82 to set the efficiency spread means "RES below the bar" is a statement about my calibration. The sweep and its crossing (SIG_A 0.215) are what stand apart from it.
6. The variance share is driven by a heavy tail of a few processes and is not weighted by economic importance.
