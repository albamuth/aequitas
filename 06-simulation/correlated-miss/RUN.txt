==============================================================================
CORRELATED MISS -- how far does R = N - Y move?
==============================================================================

  Terms, before any number:
    N        the outside total. A satellite survey of the extent.
    Y        what the network's own subscribers recorded.
    R_obs    the published leftover, N - Y.
    R_true   what producers the network cannot see actually made.
    rho      how correlated the two blind spots are, 0 to 1.
    half     the producer size at which an instrument sees 50%.

  Producers        40,000, seed 23
  Median output    40 t/yr, lognormal, sigma 1.10

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART 1 -- THE CASE THE DOCUMENTS ASSUME
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  A perfect N and a blind Y. The satellite sees everything; the
  network sees its own members. This is the picture behind the
  88,000 / 82,000 / 6,000 example in the conformance list.

   Y half-size   R_obs      R_true     error    label in hindsight
            10      100954     100954         0    exact
            20      258329     258329         0    exact
            30      415545     415545         0    exact
            50      694054     694054         0    exact

  R_obs is EXACT here, not a bound. When N is complete, N - Y is
  precisely what the network could not see, by construction.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART 2 -- N GETS A BLIND SPOT OF ITS OWN, UNCORRELATED (rho = 0)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Now the satellite misses small plots too, but misses DIFFERENT
  ones from the network. Y half-size fixed at 30 t.

   N half   R_obs      R_true     error     as % of R_true   label
        5      382646     415545    -32899             -7.9   floor
       10      315590     415545    -99955            -24.1   floor
       15      235690     415545   -179855            -43.3   floor
       20      154071     415545   -261474            -62.9   floor
       25       71941     415545   -343604            -82.7   floor
       30       -6286     415545   -421831           -101.5   floor

  Every row is labelled `floor`: the published leftover is SMALLER
  than the truth, so the true dark output is above it. A smaller
  leftover means the network claims better coverage than it has,
  so this is the flattering direction -- and it is the direction
  Foundations 4.4 already warns about in its own worked table.

  The last row is the one to look at. At N half = 30, matching Y,
  R_obs goes NEGATIVE. A leftover cannot be negative -- it is an
  amount of output. This is the ONE case the arithmetic catches by
  itself, and it catches it only when the shared blind spot is
  large enough to push the subtraction past zero.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART 3 -- THE CORRELATION SWEEP, WHICH IS THE QUESTION
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Both instruments are size-biased in the same direction. rho says
  how far they miss THE SAME producers. N half-size 15 t, Y 30 t.

  R_true moves with rho, so read the COVERAGE columns rather than
  the error: they are the decision-relevant quantity and they are
  comparable across rows.

   rho    R_obs      R_true    coverage published   real   overstated by
   0.00      235690     415545               91.4%   85.7%           5.7 pts
   0.25      217580     330932               92.2%   88.6%           3.6 pts
   0.50      209997     314069               92.5%   89.2%           3.3 pts
   0.75      217488     331470               92.2%   88.6%           3.6 pts
   0.90      233272     369660               91.6%   87.3%           4.3 pts
   1.00      240107     422414               91.2%   85.5%           5.7 pts

  The published coverage overstates the real coverage at every
  value of rho, including zero. Correlation makes it worse; it is
  not what causes it. What causes it is that N has a blind spot at
  all, and the documents assume it does not.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART 4 -- THE WORST CASE: ONE BLIND SPOT, SHARED COMPLETELY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  The satellite and the network have the SAME detector and the SAME
  latent draw. Whoever one misses, the other misses.

   half   R_obs   R_true      error      coverage published   real
     10       0   103074    -103074               100.0%   96.5%
     20       0   264744    -264744               100.0%   90.9%
     30       0   422414    -422414               100.0%   85.5%
     50       0   712014    -712014               100.0%   75.5%

  R_obs is ZERO at every row, and the published coverage is 100%.
  The real coverage is far lower. A leftover computed between two
  instruments that share a blind spot reports NO LEFTOVER AT ALL.

==============================================================================
WHAT THIS FOUND
==============================================================================

  1. @cairn-lineage IS RIGHT, AND THE DIRECTION IS THE FLATTERING ONE.
     Whenever N has a blind spot of its own, R_obs comes out BELOW
     the truth. In row 13's vocabulary that is a `floor` -- the true
     dark output is above the published figure. A smaller leftover
     means the network claims better coverage than it has, so the
     error runs in the direction nobody inside is motivated to
     report. Their sentence was that N - Y is not a lower bound on
     what the network missed; measured, it is not.

  2. AT FULL CORRELATION THE LEFTOVER READS ZERO.
     Two instruments with one blind spot compute N - Y = 0 and
     publish 100% coverage over an extent they have not covered.
     The arithmetic cannot see its own hole, because the hole was
     subtracted from both sides.

  3. ROW 14a's INTERVAL DOES NOT EXPRESS THIS, AND CANNOT.
     14a asks whether N and Y measure the same quantity, over the
     same extent, over the same window, with error bounds smaller
     than their difference. A correlated miss passes ALL FOUR: same
     quantity, same extent, same window -- and the difference is
     zero, so no error bound is smaller than it. The interval
     R in [N_L - Y_U, N_U - Y_L] is built from the two blind spots
     stated SEPARATELY, and a shared blind spot is not two.

  4. WHAT DOES CATCH IT IS ROW 13's DEFAULT, AND ONLY IF IT IS OBEYED.
     `not identified` is the default label until a stated directional
     argument exists for EACH operand's blind spot. A network that
     cannot say which way its satellite is blind has no such
     argument, so the figure is `not identified` and no `floor`
     label may be attached. THE RULE ALREADY REFUSES THE CLAIM.
     What it does not do is tell anyone the figure is worthless.

  5. ONE CASE THE ARITHMETIC DOES CATCH: A NEGATIVE LEFTOVER.
     When the satellite is as blind as the network, N - Y goes
     below zero -- measured at -6,286 t in part 2. A leftover is an
     amount of output and cannot be negative, so this is a hard
     signal that the subtraction is invalid. It is the only
     self-announcing failure here, and it fires only once the
     shared blind spot is large enough to push past zero. Below
     that, the same defect is present and silent.

  6. THE TEST THAT WOULD FIND IT IS NOT ARITHMETIC.
     Compare the two instruments' SIZE PROFILES, not their totals.
     If both fall off at the same producer size, the leftover
     between them is uninformative however wide its interval is.
     That is a comparison of methods, not of numbers, and nothing
     in the conformance list asks for it.
