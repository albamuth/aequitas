# Land is not owned; a building carries a remediation debt

> **Version:** 0.1
> **Date:** 2026-10-14

> **Land cannot be owned.** A building does not sit on property it holds title to. It occupies a bounded space relative to the Earth, and **that occupation is itself a debit: the cost of restoring the space to its natural state.** It is discharged to zero only by actually doing the restoring.

## The words this page uses

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Remediation debt** | The hours, materials and energy it would take to strip contaminants, remove the foundation and buried piping, refill the excavation, and restore native soil and wildlife on the plot. |
| **Property debit** | The debit of a thing you hold, shared among its holders by how long each held it ([property-debit](property-debit.md)). |
| **Stock rule** | A pollutant's weight floats with the current cost of putting the world back ([retroactive-reweighting](retroactive-reweighting.md)). |

## The rule

**The remediation debt is a property debit on the structure's holders**, weighted by the stock rule, and it behaves like the end-of-life debit of an object ([end-of-life-debit](end-of-life-debit.md)): it clears only by remediating.

**Two things persist whatever is done to the land.** The structure's construction and maintenance debits stay in the record forever; remediating the plot clears the *occupation* debt, not the record of what was built. And **the holder bears only what they themselves effected.** A house raised two centuries ago on unrecorded or forced labour does not put that old harm on today's occupant, who carries only what they caused while living there ([pollution-making-vs-using](pollution-making-vs-using.md)).

**Governance rides existing machinery.** The remediation cost is a mitigation-cost estimate under the stock rule and is governed like every other cost constant (Foundations §3.3a). No new capture surface, and no new answer either: it inherits OP-24 unsolved.

## An example, with the numbers

**A house has stood for 200 years. One occupant holds it for 20 of them, cooks on gas, and then hands it on.** *(All digits illustrative.)* Restoring the plot to meadow is read at **1,200 hours**.

| | Hours | Who carries it |
|---|--:|---|
| The remediation debt, in full | 1,200 h | **The structure's holders, by holding time**, until somebody restores the plot |
| This occupant's holding-time share, 20 ÷ 200 | **120 h** | This occupant, diluting as later holders accrue time |
| The gas stove's emissions over 20 years | their own measured CO₂ | **This occupant, permanently.** They burned the gas |
| The original builders' construction pollution and forced labour | on the original causers | **0 h on this occupant** |

**Now somebody restores the plot after the house comes down.** The 1,200 hours clear; the restorer is credited the hours of the restoring; the stove's CO₂ does not move, because it was consumption debit on the person who burned the gas.

**In plain words: you owe for occupying, not for owning, and you owe only for your own tenure and your own emissions.**

## Why this is bookkeeping rather than a political claim

**Owning land today is a machine for collecting rent**, which Henry George named as the capture of the gains of everybody else's work ([George_land-and-rent](../02-research/George_land-and-rent.md)). Here there is nothing to collect. Holding a plot adds to your debit and never to your credit, so holding it pays only in the use you make of it ([capitalism-cannot-function](capitalism-cannot-function.md)).

## What stays open

> **The "natural state" baseline is the hard edge.** What is the natural state of a plot that has been a city for centuries? This is the same shape as the pollution baseline, a convention with a measurable basis that is contested at the margin, and it inherits the same governance. **The mechanism is sound; the baseline convention needs specifying.** Registered as the open sub-question of Foundations §3.7.
