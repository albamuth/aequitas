# Regulator Inversion

> An EPA-like body stops being an adversary and becomes something businesses **actively want**, because it helps them lower the debit-cost of their products. Enforcement becomes consulting.

## What it is

Under conventional regulation, a firm's interest and the regulator's interest are opposed: compliance is a cost, and evasion pays. Enforcement is therefore permanently adversarial and permanently underfunded relative to the incentive to evade.

Under Aequitas the sign flips. Because [cost-not-price](cost-not-price.md) means a product's price *is* its full material cost, and because harms are priced in ([no-externalities](no-externalities.md)), anything that genuinely reduces harm reduces the product's debit-cost — which makes it more competitive.

So a body with the expertise to identify and quantify harm reduction is offering the most valuable service a producer can buy. **The regulator becomes a supplier.**

## Why it works this way

It follows from [no-externalities](no-externalities.md) alone. The adversarial relationship exists only because harm is external to the firm's accounting; once it's internal, reducing it is straightforwardly in the firm's interest.

This is one of the theory's most attractive consequences for a mainstream audience — it promises less bureaucracy and *better* environmental outcomes simultaneously, which almost nothing else does.

## Who games this

**Measurement capture.** If the same body both advises firms on lowering debit-cost and defines the weighting model that determines debit-cost, it can simply lower the number instead of the harm. This is the auditing industry's structural conflict, reproduced exactly.

The separation between *measuring* and *advising* must be specified. Linked directly to the unresolved [retroactive-reweighting](retroactive-reweighting.md) weighting-model governance problem.
