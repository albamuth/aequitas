## Why this matters to Aequitas

**This is the closest thing in history to Aequitas, and it died.** Technocracy proposed scrapping money and running the economy on *energy* instead — measuring everything in units of energy, and handing out non-tradeable coupons that expired. Aequitas has to be able to say, precisely, what it does differently and why that difference is the one that matters.

It bears on: measuring cost as physical flow ([Foundations §A1](../docs/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.17.md#a1-materialism-of-cost)); the rule that a record of your work can never be traded away ([Foundations §A3 (non-fungibility)](../docs/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.17.md#a3-non-fungibility)); the rule that no organisation owns the system's core ([Foundations §A8 (local governance)](../docs/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.17.md#a8-local-governance)); and the entire question of how a system like this ever gets adopted.

## Key findings

**What they proposed**
- A thing's worth comes from the **physical energy burned to produce it**, measured in energy units (BTUs or joules).
- Goods were handed out via **energy certificates**: you couldn't trade or gift them, and they **expired** — typically after about two years — so nobody could pile them up.
- The price system was to be abolished outright.
- The whole thing would be run by **scientists and engineers** instead of politicians (they called it "the Technate of North America").

**Why it failed** (several independent accounts agree)
- **It fell apart organisationally and personally.** The movement split into factions; its leader Howard Scott had shaky credentials and a questionable past that became a liability; his big 1933 national radio address landed badly and hurt the movement's public image.
- **It came across as elitist.** Critics found "rule by engineers" dismissive of democracy and politically naïve.
- **Its predictions flopped.** It forecast an imminent economic collapse. The collapse didn't come, and the movement's credibility went with it.
- **The method outlived the movement.** The underlying accounting survived into 1970s energy economics as *net energy analysis* / *EROI* — "energy return on energy invested," i.e. how much usable energy you get back for the energy you spend getting it. The bookkeeping was salvageable even though the movement wasn't.

## What we can use

- **A real case study for the "no organisation owns the core" rule.** Technocracy's fortunes rose and fell with one man's reputation. Aequitas's rule that no company or body may hold authority over the system isn't abstract caution — *this is the cautionary tale.* Cite it directly in any governance section.
- **A warning about adoption strategy.** Never make your case depend on a crisis. Technocracy bet on collapse and lost. Aequitas's reason-to-join has to work in normal times, for one person, today.
- **Non-tradeable value has been tried before** — it isn't an exotic idea, which is useful precedent for Aequitas's rule that credit can't change hands, even though Aequitas goes much further than expiring coupons.
- Net energy analysis / EROI is a living, respectable method with real datasets, and **might be directly usable** for costing out pollution and production. Worth its own note.

## What it gets wrong / limitations

- **One number can't carry everything.** Energy alone can't tell a joule spent healing someone from a joule spent torching a field. Aequitas tracks *several* physical dimensions at once (energy, materials, water, land, harm) and corrects them as understanding improves — which makes it *harder* to compute, not easier. Be honest about that trade rather than claiming a clean improvement.
- **Their certificates were spendable** — a currency in all but name. Making them expire is a patch against hoarding, not a real fix. Aequitas removes the need for the patch entirely: its credit is a record of *who did what*, and there is simply nothing to hand over or hoard.
- **Rule-by-experts is the exact trap Aequitas must avoid.** The danger lives on in the open question of how contribution turns into *influence* — any mechanism that converts expertise into authority risks rebuilding the Technate. (This is tracked as the project's open problem on turning contribution into say — see [Foundations §10 (open problems)](../docs/Aequitas_Foundations_v0.17.md#10-open-problems).)
- The Mises Institute source is openly hostile and should be read that way; the 2024 *Management & Organizational History* article is the better scholarly account.

## The question this note exists to force

Technocracy had a **simpler** system, real public attention, and organised funding — and it still died. So what does Aequitas have that it didn't? The defensible answers:
1. **No capturable organisation** — nobody's reputation the whole thing can rise or fall on.
2. **It works with zero technology at the bottom rung** — neighbours vouching for each other needs no infrastructure at all.
3. **A reason to join that makes sense without any crisis.**
4. **Computers**, which the 1930s did not have.

That list should be *written up and defended*, not just assumed.
