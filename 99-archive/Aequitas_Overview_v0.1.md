## 01 — Executive Summary

### Overview

Monetary systems fail to account for the true value of goods, services, and energy expenditures, and their values are highly subjective. Aequitas proposes a system that tracks all human activity and its impacts, to create an economic paradigm that still operates as a free market that ensures fairness.

### Key features and capabilities

- All work is fully credited as a nonfungible record
- Prices are equal to true value
- Cashless and immune to gambling or theft
- No costs are externalized
- Dynamic values are inflation-proof
- Regulated by Direct Democracy
- Allows for Private Property
- Discourages Consumerism
- Eradicates Capitalism
- Replaces Taxation
- Encourages Education, Science, and the Arts
- Obsoletes Nation-States
- Minimizes Economic Crime and Wealth Inequity

### Benefits

**Production:**
By rethinking what we conceive of as "value", workers will always be credited with the full worth of their labor as a unit of time, and goods can be broken down into all the time, material, and energy made to produce, transport, and distribute it. We can factor in previously externalized costs such as pollution, free resource extraction, and exploitative labor practices to create a true cost for goods and services. Private property can still exist, however the debt incurred by owning it will discourage hoarding and excessive wealth inequity. People that do not work at all can still live comfortably because basic goods and services provided to them will still be accounted for, without having to create a "welfare state."

**Service:**
Labor such as therapy, nursing, waitstaff, or any other kind of work that doesn't create a physical object can be accounted for. We can credit and assign value to previously uncredited work, such as housework and child-rearing, getting an education, and volunteering. Since the education and training required to work in fields such as medical care, construction, or information technology will be a credited activity, this transfers the Human Capital Investment cost from the individual to society as a whole. Since the recipients of the service do not "pay" the provider of the service, providers are freed to provide services to anyone. The debts recipients of service incur are fairly assigned, creating a truly egalitarian system.

**Enrichment:**
Work that provides an intangible benefit to human society as a whole, such as art, entertainment, scientific discovery, education, and such is also credited. Using feedback models inspired by social media and peer review, outstanding creators and discoverers will be awarded greater resources and value-creation authority to do more. This creates a true meritocracy where outstanding achievements are rewarded and encouraged.

## 02 — Problem: Inequality

*The age-old problem of wealth concentration.*

Many of the root problems at the core of society and thus political conflict arise from the way that humanity views ownership and thus the very concept of wealth. Wealth as we see it starts with the basic needs of human beings being met for survival, then goods and services that liberate people and give them freedom to choose the life they want, and culminates with luxuries that only bring pleasure. The Aequitas system is an attempt to give humanity all of these things without a prescribed or planned economy administered by a State or authoritarian regime.

For example, conservatives often blame external actors (immigrants, rival nation-states, regulations on business) for the misery of their people, while liberals blame social policies (taxation inequity, government services, infrastructure). Leftists point to systemic issues (capitalism, racism, class hierarchy, patriarchy). In all these analyses, the conflict is about who controls the resources, and how they are distributed.

All of these analyses assume that the way we think of ownership, property, and goods cannot change. Philosophers like Thomas Hobbes and David Hume argued that in a state of nature (without government), property rights are meaningless because anyone can take what you possess by force. Property, therefore, is not a natural right but a *social convention* or a *positive right* created and enforced by the state or sovereign power to maintain peace and order.

Entrenched private property rights create and perpetuate systemic inequality, functioning less as a tool of individual freedom and more as a mechanism of social power. The wealthy use property as capital to accumulate more wealth and political influence, which can then be used to enforce laws that further protect their own property interests, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of wealth concentration.

The logical conclusion for materialist philosophers like Marx is that abolishing private ownership of the means of production and instituting **common ownership** (or social ownership) is necessary to align the system of appropriation with the social reality of production. Unfortunately, this requires a forceful redistribution of ownership, and the owners have always resisted and worked against any such ownership. States have tried and failed to redistribute wealth, such as Communist Russia and China, inevitably giving rise to a new oligarchy and the same wealth inequities, since any concentration of power creates corruption.

Jean-Jacques Rousseau famously criticized the entire concept of ownership in his *Discourse on Inequality*, suggesting that the man who first fenced off a piece of land and declared, "This is mine," was the true founder of civil society, and thus, the source of social inequality. Many traditional or Indigenous worldviews reject the Western notion of owning land or resources. The relationship is often viewed as one of stewardship or custodianship, where humans have a duty to care for the earth, but not an exclusive right of ownership over it.

Anarcho-Communists reject all forms of property, based on the belief that **scarcity is often artificially enforced** by property rights, and that ownership creates hierarchy, class divisions, and state power. Instead of ownership, the principle is **"use-right"** or **"possession"**, whereby a person "possesses" their home while they live in it and their tools while they work with them. But they do not "own" them in a legal or proprietary sense that allows them to leave the house vacant while others are homeless or to refuse to share specialized tools that are not currently in use.

### Solution: Debit

*Reframing possessions as debits rather than as positive assets.*

Take for example a person: we can see them eating food, wearing clothes, walking to work, riding in a train or car, buying a sandwich and throwing the wrapper in the garbage, and going to sleep in an apartment or in a tent on the sidewalk, maybe taking a shower if they can afford it, and using the toilet. In all these activities, air is going into their lungs and back out, slightly transformed. Food is being ingested, digested, and excrement is coming out. That food is grown somewhere and shipped, then processed into a sandwich, packed in plastic, transported on a truck burning gasoline or running off of batteries charged with wind energy, and put on the shelf. After eating the sandwich, the plastic wrapper goes into a landfill or the ocean, leaching microplastics into the water table for a thousand years. At some point in our future, perhaps, robots will come along and clean the microplastics out of the water and soil, but for today, those microplastics and the emissions of the truck that moved the sandwich remain with us. And let's not forget the labor of the mechanic that fixed the truck's leaky oil filter, nor the toxic chemicals washed into the water table because of the leaky oil filter, or the pollution from fertilizers used to grow the wheat to make the sandwich.

This example highlights the interconnectedness of material things of contemporary society, what it costs to make them, and even **externalized costs** like pollution that comes from creating and consuming those things. Aequitas aims to account for all such costs, calling them **"debits"**.

Most debt is easily accounted for, if we look at the supply chain. We can know how much wheat the farmer grows in a season, how much of the grain is harvested, how much is lost due to spillage or rot, how much irrigation water went into it, and how much ended up in the bread that made the sandwich. We can also extrapolate debt like carbon emissions by estimating the product's contribution, and the estimated cost of mitigating that pollution.

## 03 — Problem: Debt

*The current world economy depends on ever-increasing debt to stimulate growth.*

> "The debt supercycle is the product of an ever-increasing buildup of borrowing by consumers and governments. For example, total debt was about 140 percent of GDP between 1960 and 1980 in the United States, but has since more than doubled—to 300 percent of GDP. The same trend holds true globally. In fact, not even the Great Recession of 2008—which in many ways was a result of the excesses of borrowing—could put a dent in debt's relentless upward march.
>
> "What dangers does the debt supercycle pose to the world economy? An economy that relies on a constant supply of new debt to generate demand is always susceptible to disruptions in financial markets, which can trigger serious slowdowns. This is what happened in 2008 with household debt. Since then, the economy has relied more on government debt to generate demand. Governments in advanced economies can often borrow at a rate lower than their rate of growth, which makes it easier for them to sustain the debt supercycle and keep the economy afloat. But dependence on continuous government borrowing is politically risky because it relies on continued financial market stability." *(Source cited in original)*

## Appendix A — Lineage: Aequitas is the successor to OFCS

Aequitas builds on the **Open Fair Credit Standard (OFCS)** (earlier: "Open Free Credit System"). Key inherited concepts, preserved here for continuity:

- **Not a single system, but a standard** — a set of requirements, not one software tool or accounting method.
- **Three scientific-theory criteria:** universality, testability (decentralization), fecundity.
- **Universal standard of value:** must be the same everywhere, measurable anywhere, and form the basis for derivative values (e.g. time in seconds, energy in joules, mass in grams).
- **Currency vs. Credit:** Currency carries little information, is owner-less, fluctuates, depends on a single issuer, represents redeemable value. Credit is *only* information, exclusively owned, absolute, issuable by anyone, and is its own value.
- **Full Cost Accounting:** cost measured in Time, Energy, Materials — evaluated over the whole life-cycle: Creation/Transportation (past), Usage (present), Disposal (future).
- **Free Market vs. Capitalism:** the free market means *you* decide how to spend the value of your labor; capitalism (private ownership of capital goods) tends to aggregate into hierarchies and is therefore *inherently anti-free-market*.
- **Rollout model:** OFCS-based **credit syndicates** start locally (like local currencies / Transition Towns), grow, communicate, merge by reconciling rules, and evolve via free-market selection until legacy currencies become obsolete.
- **Account model:** each account holds total earned credits (work done) and total accrued debits (owned/consumed items). Credit is immediate and permanent once verified; cannot be transferred or stolen; debit/credit ratio rules customized per syndicate.
